## 1. The Structural Hierarchy: Beat > Scene > Sequence > Act > Story

Every level of story structure obeys the same principle: a series of smaller units build through escalating conflict to a turning point (climax) at that level, which then becomes one unit in the next level up.

### How the levels nest

- **Beats** (exchanges of action/reaction) build a **scene** that culminates in a scene turning point -- a minor but significant value change.
- **Scenes** (typically 2-5) build a **sequence** that culminates in a sequence climax -- a moderate value change with greater impact than any single scene.
- **Sequences** (typically 2-4) build an **act** that culminates in an act climax -- a major reversal, greater than any sequence climax.
- **Acts** (minimum three for a full-length work) build the **story**, culminating in the Story Climax -- the most irreversible change of all.

### Key principle: escalation at every level

At each level, the climax must produce a change greater in magnitude than the climax of the level below it. A sequence climax is more impactful than any of its scene climaxes. An act climax is more impactful than any of its sequence climaxes. The Story Climax is the greatest reversal of all.

**Checklist -- Structural Escalation:**
- [ ] Does every scene produce a value change (however minor)?
- [ ] Does the sequence climax hit harder than any individual scene within that sequence?
- [ ] Does the act climax produce a bigger reversal than any sequence climax within that act?
- [ ] Does the Story Climax produce the most irreversible, highest-stakes turning point of the entire narrative?

## 2. The Law of Conflict

McKee states a foundational rule: nothing moves forward in a story except through conflict. Conflict is not merely an aesthetic preference -- it is what carries the audience through time without awareness of time passing. When conflict disappears, the audience becomes conscious of duration, and engagement collapses.

### Practical implications

- Every scene must contain conflict. If a scene lacks conflict, it stalls the story regardless of how beautiful the prose or setting may be.
- Conflict held on pause too long causes the audience to disengage. Brief pauses create rhythm; extended pauses lose readers.
- Conflict is rooted in scarcity -- there is never enough of anything (time, love, justice, resources) for characters to have what they want without struggle.

**Checklist -- Conflict Audit:**
- [ ] Does every scene contain active conflict (not just tension or mood)?
- [ ] Is the source of conflict rooted in genuine scarcity or competing desires?
- [ ] If a scene has no overt conflict, is there subtext-level conflict driving the exchange?
- [ ] Are any scenes merely expository or atmospheric without a conflict engine? If so, cut or restructure them.

## 3. Complication vs. Complexity

McKee draws a critical distinction between two ways to build conflict across a story.

### Complication (conflict on one level only)

A story is merely "complicated" when all conflict sits on a single level:

| Conflict Level | Genre Examples |
|---|---|
| Extra-personal only (society, environment, physical forces) | Action/Adventure, Farce, Horror |
| Personal only (relationships, family, friends, lovers) | Soap Opera, Domestic Drama |
| Inner only (mind, emotion, body) | Stream of Consciousness, literary prose |

Complicated stories have two hallmarks: (1) a large cast to generate enough conflict from a single level, and (2) many locations and sets to keep varying the surface.

### Complexity (conflict on all three levels simultaneously)

A complex story places the protagonist in conflict on inner, personal, AND extra-personal levels -- often at the same time within a single scene.

McKee illustrates this with the French toast scene from KRAMER VS. KRAMER: Kramer faces inner conflict (self-confidence, male arrogance vs. fear), personal conflict (his son's distress, the broken trust of an abandoned child), and extra-personal conflict (the hostile kitchen, burning grease, spilled milk -- the physical world fighting him). All three levels turn from positive to negative in a three-minute scene.

### Design guideline

For most fiction, aim for complexity over complication. Keep the cast and world reasonably contained, and concentrate on building conflict across multiple levels simultaneously. As McKee advises: do not proliferate characters or multiply locations; instead, discipline yourself to a contained cast and world while building rich complexity across levels.

**Checklist -- Complexity Audit:**
- [ ] Does the protagonist face inner conflict (competing desires, fears, moral dilemmas)?
- [ ] Does the protagonist face personal conflict (relationships under stress)?
- [ ] Does the protagonist face extra-personal conflict (institutional, environmental, societal forces)?
- [ ] Are at least two levels active in most major scenes?
- [ ] In key climactic scenes, are all three levels converging?

## 4. Sequence Design

A sequence is a series of scenes (typically 2-5) that builds through escalating conflict to a moderate turning point -- the sequence climax.

### What makes a sequence (vs. a random collection of scenes)

- The scenes within a sequence share a common dramatic concern or tactical problem the protagonist is trying to solve.
- Each successive scene raises the stakes or changes the approach after the previous scene's outcome proved insufficient.
- The sequence climax produces a value change of greater magnitude than any individual scene climax within it.

### Designing a sequence

1. **Identify the sequence-level objective.** What is the character trying to achieve or resolve across this cluster of scenes?
2. **Plan 2-5 scenes that escalate.** Each scene should attempt a different tactic or face a new obstacle. No scene should repeat the action type or intensity of the previous one.
3. **Build to the sequence climax.** The final scene of the sequence should produce a turning point that is clearly more significant than the minor changes in earlier scenes.
4. **Ensure the sequence climax feeds the act.** The outcome of the sequence climax should set up or complicate the next sequence, building toward the act climax.

**Checklist -- Sequence Health:**
- [ ] Can you state the dramatic question the sequence is answering?
- [ ] Does each scene within the sequence escalate -- either in stakes, emotional cost, or tactical desperation?
- [ ] Is the sequence climax clearly the most impactful moment in the sequence?
- [ ] Does the sequence climax create a new situation that propels the next sequence forward?
- [ ] Are there any scenes within the sequence that could be cut without losing the escalation arc?

## 5. Act Design

An act is a series of sequences that builds to a major turning point -- the act climax -- which creates a reversal so significant it reshapes the trajectory of the entire story.

### The three-act minimum

McKee argues that for a full-length work (novel, feature film, full-length play), three acts is the structural minimum. The reasoning:

- Two major reversals are not enough to take an audience to the limits of experience. A story with only two turning points (good-then-bad, bad-then-good) feels incomplete -- we sense there should be more.
- Three major reversals create a rhythm that satisfies: the third turn pushes beyond expectation and reaches the boundary of the story's world.
- The three-act structure is a foundation, not a formula. Stories can have more acts, but rarely fewer (for full-length works).

### Act proportions (approximate)

- **Act One:** roughly 25% of the telling. Opens with (or builds to) the Inciting Incident and climaxes with the first major reversal.
- **Act Two:** the longest act, roughly 50% or more. Contains the bulk of progressive complications. This is where the "long second act" problem lives.
- **Act Three:** the shortest act. Delivers a sense of acceleration -- swift rising action to the Story Climax. Keep it brief; stretching the last act kills momentum.

### Solving the long second act

The middle of a story is the hardest to sustain. McKee identifies two solutions:

1. **Add subplots.** Weave subplot act climaxes into the middle of the Central Plot's second act to keep major reversals coming.
2. **Use a Mid-Act Climax.** Build to a major reversal at the midpoint of Act Two, effectively splitting it and creating a four-act rhythm. This accelerates pacing in the middle of the story.

McKee notes that stories can expand to five, seven, or even eight acts (as in RAIDERS OF THE LOST ARK), but the more acts you add, the more brilliant scenes you must invent -- and the multiplication of act climaxes reduces the impact of each individual one while increasing the risk of repetition.

**Checklist -- Act Design:**
- [ ] Does each act climax produce a major reversal -- a change clearly greater than any sequence climax within the act?
- [ ] Does the first act climax occur roughly 20-25% into the story?
- [ ] Is the second act sustained by either subplots or a midpoint reversal (or both)?
- [ ] Is the final act the shortest, creating a sense of acceleration toward the Story Climax?
- [ ] Does each act climax change the direction of the story in a way that cannot be undone?

## 6. Progressive Complications

Progressive complications are the engine of rising action. Every obstacle, conflict, or setback the protagonist encounters must be greater than the last in terms of external pressure, internal cost, or both.

### The anti-progressive trap

If Act Two gives the characters actions of the same size or kind as Act One, the story is treading water. The audience senses that if lesser actions did not work in Act One, equivalent actions will not work in Act Two. The result is repetition, not progression.

### How to build progressive complications

1. **Escalate external stakes.** Each new obstacle should be harder to overcome, demand more from the protagonist, and carry greater consequences for failure.
2. **Escalate internal cost.** Each action the protagonist takes should require a greater moral, emotional, or psychological sacrifice than the last.
3. **Never repeat an action type at the same intensity.** If a character tried persuasion in one scene, the next scene should require a different tactic, or a more desperate form of the same tactic.
4. **Invent abundantly.** McKee advises generating far more scenes than you need (he mentions inventing hundreds of potential scenes for a feature-length work, then selecting the forty to sixty best). If you devise only the exact number you need, the work is almost certain to become anti-progressive and repetitive.

### The quantity rule

A feature-length story typically requires forty to sixty scenes organized into twelve to eighteen sequences across three or more acts. To avoid repetition, the writer must brainstorm many more scenes than needed, then select only the strongest -- those that produce the most memorable turning points.

**Checklist -- Progressive Complications:**
- [ ] Does each scene's conflict or obstacle surpass the previous one in difficulty, risk, or emotional cost?
- [ ] Has the protagonist exhausted lesser options before being forced to more extreme actions?
- [ ] Are there any adjacent scenes that repeat the same tactic at the same intensity level? If so, cut or restructure.
- [ ] Have you generated significantly more scene ideas than you need, selecting only the best?
- [ ] Does the escalation feel organic to the character's situation, not arbitrary or manufactured?

## 7. Act Rhythm and Value-Charge Alternation

The emotional power of a story depends on the alternation of value charges at act climaxes.

### The core rule

The last two act climaxes of a story must carry opposite charges. If the Story Climax is positive (protagonist achieves desire), the Penultimate Act Climax must be negative. If the Story Climax is negative, the Penultimate must be positive. This is non-negotiable.

- **Up-ending:** Penultimate = negative, Story Climax = positive. (Not: positive then more positive.)
- **Down-ending:** Penultimate = positive, Story Climax = negative. (Not: negative then more negative.)
- **Ironic ending:** The Story Climax is both positive and negative. The Penultimate Climax should contradict the overall lean of the irony. Study the irony carefully to determine whether it leans positive or negative overall, then design the Penultimate to oppose that lean.

When emotional experience repeats (two positives or two negatives in a row at the act level), the power of the second event is halved.

### Working backward from the ending

McKee recommends designing act climaxes from the end backward. Once the Story Climax is determined, set the Penultimate to the opposite charge, then work backward through earlier acts, allowing subplot and sequence climaxes to create a varied rhythm in between.

**Checklist -- Act Rhythm:**
- [ ] Do the final two act climaxes carry opposite emotional charges?
- [ ] Does the rhythm of all act climaxes avoid consecutive same-charge reversals?
- [ ] If the ending is ironic, does the Penultimate Climax contradict the irony's overall lean?
- [ ] Between act climaxes, do subplot and sequence climaxes create varied emotional rhythms?

## 8. False Endings

A False Ending is a scene (usually near the Penultimate Act Climax or within the last act's movement) that is so seemingly complete the audience momentarily believes the story is over.

### When to use a False Ending

- Most commonly in Action genres and thrillers, where the villain or threat appears decisively defeated, only for a final escalation to reveal otherwise.
- McKee illustrates this with ALIEN (Ripley destroys the ship, seemingly safe), TERMINATOR (a double false ending -- the Terminator appears destroyed twice before the final confrontation), and BRAZIL (the lovers embrace in apparent escape from tyranny -- or is it?).

### Design caution

- For most stories, the False Ending is inappropriate. Use it only in genres where physical threat and survival are central.
- The Penultimate Act Climax should intensify the story's Major Dramatic Question, not feel like a premature answer to it.

## 9. Subplot Design and Integration

### The four subplot relationships

McKee identifies four ways subplots can relate to the Central Plot:

#### 9a. Contradiction (adds irony)

The subplot contradicts the Controlling Idea of the Central Plot. If the Central Plot ends positively on a theme (e.g., love triumphs through sacrifice), the subplot ends negatively on the same theme (e.g., love destroyed by possessiveness). This enriches the work with irony and moral complexity.

#### 9b. Resonance (reinforces theme with variation)

The subplot expresses the same Controlling Idea as the Central Plot, but in a different context, tone, or mode. This creates thematic variation that strengthens the central theme through multiple angles. McKee illustrates this with A MIDSUMMER NIGHT'S DREAM, where multiple love stories all end happily but through different tones -- sweet, farcical, sublime.

#### 9c. Complication (adds difficulty for protagonist)

The most important relationship: the subplot functions as an additional source of antagonism that makes the protagonist's Central Plot pursuit more difficult. McKee notes this is often the Love Story subplot in genres like Crime or Thriller -- the romance does not merely exist alongside the main plot but actively complicates it by creating competing obligations, emotional vulnerabilities, or divided attention.

#### 9d. Setup (opens the storytelling for a late-arriving Central Plot)

When the Central Plot's Inciting Incident is delayed (arriving 25-30+ minutes in), a setup subplot fills the opening, engaging the audience while establishing the protagonist and world. The setup subplot dramatizes the Central Plot's exposition indirectly so it is absorbed fluidly.

### Subplot design rules

- **A subplot must relate to the Central Plot thematically or structurally.** If it merely runs alongside without contradicting, resonating with, or complicating the Central Plot, it will split the story and destroy unity. The audience instinctively knows every story element exists because of its relationship to every other element.
- **Subplots have their own act structure**, usually abbreviated. A subplot might have one, two, or three acts of its own, with its own Inciting Incident, climaxes, and (sometimes) resolution.
- **Subplot climaxes fill the gaps between Central Plot act climaxes.** Their major reversals keep the middle of the story eventful and sustain audience engagement through the long second act.
- **Control emphasis carefully.** If the subplot begins to demand more focus and empathy than the Central Plot, reconsider: perhaps the subplot should become the Central Plot. If you keep it as a subplot, deemphasize some of its elements (its Inciting Incident, climaxes, or resolution can be kept offscreen or understated).
- **If subplot and Central Plot share the same protagonist,** the subplot complicates the protagonist's inner life and creates competing pressures. If they have different protagonists, be careful not to draw too much empathy toward the subplot protagonist at the expense of the main one.

**Checklist -- Subplot Integration:**
- [ ] Can you identify which of the four relationships (contradiction, resonance, complication, setup) each subplot has to the Central Plot?
- [ ] Does every subplot connect thematically or structurally to the Central Plot?
- [ ] Do subplot climaxes fall between Central Plot act climaxes, sustaining momentum in the middle?
- [ ] Is the emphasis between Central Plot and subplot properly balanced (Central Plot dominant)?
- [ ] If a subplot has no clear relationship to the Central Plot, should it be cut or restructured?

## 10. The Midpoint (Mid-Act Climax)

The Mid-Act Climax is a major reversal placed at the midpoint of the story (center of Act Two in a three-act structure). It effectively splits the long second act into two halves, creating a four-movement rhythm.

### Purpose

- Solves the "long second act" problem by inserting a major turning point where the story is most likely to sag.
- Accelerates mid-story pacing by giving the audience a significant reversal when they most need one.
- Expands the three-act foundation into a four-act design (similar to the Ibsen model).

### Design guidance

- The midpoint reversal should be a genuine act-level turning point, not merely a sequence climax inflated to seem bigger than it is.
- It should redirect the story -- after the midpoint, the protagonist's situation, understanding, or strategy must be fundamentally different.
- McKee cites SHINE, where David's collapse after performing Rachmaninoff's Piano Concerto No. 3 serves as a midpoint that reshapes the entire second half.

**Checklist -- Midpoint Design:**
- [ ] Does the midpoint produce a reversal significant enough to qualify as an act climax (not just a sequence climax)?
- [ ] Does it redirect the story so the protagonist's situation is fundamentally altered?
- [ ] Does it fall roughly at the halfway point of the overall narrative?
- [ ] Does it divide the long middle section into two halves, each with its own sense of escalation?

## 11. Design Variations

Stories vary in their structural patterns. McKee identifies several axes of variation:

### By number of acts

- **Miniplot:** one or two acts (short stories, one-act plays, experimental film).
- **Standard Archplot:** three or four acts with subplots (most novels, feature films, full-length plays).
- **Action/genre heavy:** seven or eight acts (many action films).
- **Multiplot:** a dozen or more turning points distributed across multiple plotlines with no single Central Plot (e.g., SHORT CUTS, THE JOY LUCK CLUB).

### By Inciting Incident placement

- **Conventional:** Inciting Incident early (within the first 10-15%), building to Act One Climax at the 25% mark.
- **Late-arriving:** Inciting Incident delayed to the 25-30% mark, where it effectively serves double duty as both Inciting Incident and Act One Climax. Requires a setup subplot to fill the opening. (ROCKY, CASABLANCA, CHINATOWN)

### By convention-breaking

McKee emphasizes: you are free to break or bend any convention, but only if you replace it with something more compelling. Every deviation from expected structure must serve the story -- convention is broken for one reason only: to put something more important in its place.

## 12. Multiplot Design

A Multiplot has no Central Plot spine. Instead, it weaves multiple story lines of roughly equal weight, unified not by a single protagonist's journey but by a shared idea or theme.

### Characteristics

- Plot lines cross-cut between each other or connect via a shared motif, object, or setting.
- Unity comes from thematic coherence: each plotline plays a variation on the same controlling idea.
- The result frames an image of a community, culture, or social landscape rather than following a single character's arc.

McKee illustrates this with PARENTHOOD (variations on the impossibility of winning at parenthood), DINER (variations on the inability of men to communicate with women), and DO THE RIGHT THING (the universality of urban racism).

### Design advantage

Multiplot gives the writer the ability to capture the portrait of a community with ample narrative drive, combining the cultural scope of static literary fiction with the forward momentum of plot-driven storytelling.

## 13. Summary: Master Principles for the AI Writing Agent

### The five non-negotiable rules

1. **Nothing moves forward except through conflict.** Every scene must have it. Every sequence must build it. Every act must climax with a major expression of it.

2. **Escalation is mandatory at every level.** Beat-to-beat, scene-to-scene, sequence-to-sequence, act-to-act -- each turning point must surpass the last in stakes, difficulty, or emotional cost.

3. **Act climaxes must alternate in value charge.** Consecutive same-charge act climaxes halve each other's power. The final two must be opposite.

4. **Subplots must connect to the Central Plot.** Every subplot must contradict, resonate with, complicate, or set up the Central Plot. A disconnected subplot destroys unity.

5. **Complexity over complication.** Build conflict across multiple levels (inner, personal, extra-personal) simultaneously rather than multiplying characters and locations on a single level.

### The overarching design process

1. Determine the Story Climax (the final, most powerful reversal).
2. Set the Penultimate Act Climax to the opposite charge.
3. Work backward through earlier acts, placing act climaxes that create a varied rhythm.
4. Within each act, design sequences that escalate toward the act climax.
5. Within each sequence, design scenes that escalate toward the sequence climax.
6. Weave subplots so their climaxes fill gaps between Central Plot act climaxes.
7. Audit everything for progressive complications: no repetition of action type or intensity.
