# Story Structure Templates

Concrete frameworks that can be applied to specific stories. Each template provides scaffolding for agents to fill in story-specific details and test story logic.

---

## Three-Act Archplot (Classical Structure)

The dominant form in commercial fiction and film. This structure emphasizes clear causality, active protagonists, and closed endings.

### Full Template with Beating Points

**ACT ONE: Setup (20-25% of total length)**

1. **Opening Image:** [A vivid, specific scene establishing the protagonist's ordinary life before disruption. Tone, world, and baseline values are introduced.]
   - Length: 1-5 pages / 1,000-5,000 words
   - Function: Immersion and contrast
   - Should mirror/contrast with final image

2. **Exposition:** [Introduction of character, world, and situation. Establish what's normal, what's at stake, and why we should care about this protagonist.]
   - Length: 5-15 pages / 5,000-15,000 words
   - Function: Establish baseline and raise questions
   - Plant information the audience needs

3. **Inciting Incident:** [An event that shatters the protagonist's status quo. Irreversible, raises the Major Dramatic Question, creates both conscious and unconscious desire.]
   - Length: 1-3 pages / 1,000-3,000 words
   - Function: Launch the story
   - Should occur by 25% mark at latest

4. **Debate/Refusal:** [The protagonist hesitates, refuses the call, or debates whether to engage. Creates doubt and raises stakes.]
   - Length: 2-5 pages / 2,000-5,000 words
   - Function: Establish protagonist's reluctance and raise tension
   - Shows why the journey is difficult

**ACT TWO: Rising Action (50-60% of total length)**

5. **Act One Climax:** [Final major turning point of Act One. A choice or revelation that commits the protagonist to the journey. No turning back.]
   - Length: 1-3 pages / 1,000-3,000 words
   - Function: Commit protagonist to action
   - Must be more impactful than earlier turning points

6. **B Story Introduction:** [Introduction of a character or relationship that represents the thematic argument of the story. Often an emotional or moral mirror.]
   - Length: 1-3 pages / 1,000-3,000 words
   - Function: Establish emotional/thematic context
   - B Story character challenges or supports A Story values

7. **Rising Action Sequence One:** [Initial complications arise from the inciting incident. Protagonist pursues their conscious desire. Obstacles emerge.]
   - Length: 5-10 pages / 5,000-10,000 words
   - Function: Establish the central conflict
   - Multiple action-reaction cycles (beats)
   - First test of protagonist's commitment

8. **Rising Action Sequence Two:** [Complications intensify. Stakes rise. Protagonist's approach proves insufficient. Effort escalates.]
   - Length: 5-10 pages / 5,000-10,000 words
   - Function: Deepen conflict and raise stakes
   - Gaps between expectation and result
   - Introduce new antagonistic forces

9. **Midpoint Reversal:** [A major turning point at the story's center (around 50% mark). Either false victory (protagonist seems to win but faces greater obstacles) or false defeat (protagonist seems lost but gains new hope/information).]
   - Length: 2-5 pages / 2,000-5,000 words
   - Function: Raise stakes and shift protagonist's understanding
   - Reorient audience expectations
   - Often reveals new information or antagonistic forces

10. **B Story Development:** [The B Story character/relationship deepens. Thematic argument becomes clearer. Often a moment of emotional truth or conflict in this relationship.]
    - Length: 2-4 pages / 2,000-4,000 words
    - Function: Deepen thematic resonance
    - Often shows what the protagonist is learning (or refusing to learn)

11. **Rising Action Sequence Three:** [After midpoint, complications continue with higher stakes. Protagonist's old methods increasingly fail. Personal cost escalates.]
    - Length: 5-10 pages / 5,000-10,000 words
    - Function: Test protagonist at deeper level
    - Moral, emotional, or psychological stakes rise
    - Antagonistic forces prove more formidable

12. **Rising Action Sequence Four:** [Final complications before the crisis. Protagonist is pushed to limits. External and internal pressure simultaneous.]
    - Length: 5-10 pages / 5,000-10,000 words
    - Function: Maximum pressure before climax
    - All avenues seem exhausted
    - Protagonist sees no good options

13. **Act Two Climax (All Is Lost / False Victory):** [The point of maximum tension. Either the protagonist appears to have lost everything (All Is Lost), or wins a Pyrrhic victory that doesn't solve the central problem (False Victory).]
    - Length: 2-4 pages / 2,000-4,000 words
    - Function: Move into final act
    - Emotional low point or illusory high point
    - Raises question: How will protagonist recover/proceed?

**ACT THREE: Resolution (15-20% of total length)**

14. **Crisis:** [The protagonist faces a true dilemma. Two irreconcilable goods or evils. No perfect solution. Must choose between unsatisfying options.]
    - Length: 1-3 pages / 1,000-3,000 words
    - Function: Force protagonist's ultimate choice
    - Clarify what the story is truly about
    - Test protagonist's character against highest stakes

15. **B Story Resolution:** [The B Story reaches climax. Often shows the protagonist's transformation or refusal of transformation. Emotional payoff of thematic journey.]
    - Length: 1-2 pages / 1,000-2,000 words
    - Function: Thematic resolution
    - May parallel or contrast with A Story climax

16. **Climax:** [The protagonist takes an irreversible action in response to the crisis. This action, taken under maximum pressure, directly causes absolute change in the story world.]
    - Length: 2-5 pages / 2,000-5,000 words
    - Function: Resolve central conflict
    - Protagonist is active; consciously chooses
    - Consequences are irreversible

17. **Falling Action:** [Consequences of the climactic action reverberate. Loose ends tie off. Secondary conflicts resolve. World adjusts to the change protagonist's action caused.]
    - Length: 3-5 pages / 3,000-5,000 words
    - Function: Wind down from maximum tension
    - Show effects of climactic choice
    - Resolve lingering tensions

18. **Resolution:** [The new status quo after climax. Emotional decompression. Thematic payoff clearly established.]
    - Length: 2-4 pages / 2,000-4,000 words
    - Function: Provide closure and meaning
    - Show how the world has changed
    - Emotional and thematic completion

19. **Final Image:** [Mirror or contrast with opening image. Shows transformation or stasis. Last image the reader carries away.]
    - Length: 1-3 pages / 1,000-3,000 words
    - Function: Thematic punctuation
    - Bookend the story
    - Concentrated meaning in image

### Recommended Proportions

- Opening Image to Act One Climax: 20-25% of total
- Rising Action (Act 1 Climax to Act 2 Climax): 50-60% of total
- Crisis to Final Image: 15-20% of total

Each midpoint and climax must be more impactful than what preceded it.

### Best Suited For

- Commercial fiction (thriller, action, mystery, romance)
- Most screenplays and teleplays
- Genre fiction with clear antagonistic forces
- Stories where plot and character arc are equally important
- Audiences expecting clear resolution and satisfying closure

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Inciting Incident Too Late:** If the inciting incident occurs after 25% of the story, exposition becomes unbearable and audience engagement lags
- **Weak Midpoint:** A midpoint that doesn't genuinely shift understanding or stakes makes Act Two sag in the middle
- **Act Two Sag:** Repetitive complications that don't escalate; obstacles that don't feel progressively harder
- **Insufficient Crisis:** A "dilemma" that actually has a clear right answer isn't a true crisis; it won't feel earned if climax follows easily
- **Passive Climax:** If something happens *to* the protagonist rather than the protagonist making an active choice, the climax feels like accident rather than culmination
- **Rushed Resolution:** Cramming too much into Act Three. The climax is the story; resolution should be brief emotional decompression
- **Contradictory Final Image:** A final image that contradicts or ignores what the climax established undermines the story's meaning

## Five-Act Structure

A traditional theatrical structure with five major movements. Often used in classical drama, Shakespearean plays, and epic literature.

### Template

1. **Exposition:** [Establish character, world, situation, and relationships. Introduce the protagonist and their ordinary state.]
   - Function: Foundation and investment
   - Establishes what's at stake

2. **Rising Action:** [Introduction of conflict. Events escalate in complexity and consequence. Protagonist pursues goals; obstacles mount. Multiple turning points build tension.]
   - Function: Develop conflict; raise stakes progressively
   - Complications arise from character choice and circumstance
   - Each event raises question: What happens next?

3. **Climax (Turning Point):** [The peak moment of tension. The major dramatic question is about to be answered. Protagonist faces ultimate test or revelation.]
   - Function: Pivot point of the entire story
   - Cannot be undone
   - Shifts everything that follows

4. **Falling Action:** [Consequences of the climax reverberate. Secondary characters react. The protagonist begins to understand what has changed. Loose ends tighten.]
   - Function: Clarify consequences and prepare for resolution
   - Show how the climactic action changes everything
   - Build toward final revelation

5. **Catastrophe/Resolution:** [The final state after climax. New equilibrium established. Thematic meaning made clear. Often includes death, transformation, or profound change.]
   - Function: Final emotional and thematic payoff
   - Show what the story meant
   - Provide closure or open-ended reflection

### Best Suited For

- Classical drama and Shakespearean plays
- Epic narratives with multiple story threads
- Historical fiction and period pieces
- Philosophical or thematic narratives where meaning unfolds gradually
- Stories with ensemble casts where multiple character arcs interweave

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Unclear Act Divisions:** If audiences can't identify where acts break, pacing feels muddled
- **Weak Turning Point:** A climax that doesn't genuinely shift the story's direction makes falling action feel aimless
- **Overloaded Resolution:** Too many revelations or turns in the final act; audience can't absorb all change at once
- **Repetitive Rising Action:** Similar obstacles repeatedly; sense of progression lost

## The Hero's Journey (Campbell/Vogler)

Joseph Campbell's monomyth, popularized by Christopher Vogler. A universal pattern found across mythology and modern storytelling. The template maps internal and external journeys simultaneously.

### Full Template

1. **Ordinary World:** [The hero's life before the adventure. Establish normalcy, limitations, and what's missing. Often hints at why the hero is ready for transformation, even if they don't know it.]
   - What is the hero's ordinary life?
   - What are their limitations or unfulfilled desires?
   - What's the emotional baseline before disruption?

2. **Call to Adventure:** [An event or message summoning the hero to adventure. Often resisted. May seem trivial at first.]
   - What summons the hero?
   - Why now? (timing matters)
   - What does the hero think they're being called to do?

3. **Refusal of the Call:** [The hero hesitates, refuses, or expresses doubt. Shows the cost of the journey and why stepping into the unknown is terrifying.]
   - Why is the hero reluctant?
   - What do they fear losing?
   - What's at stake in refusing vs. accepting?

4. **Meeting the Mentor:** [A figure (or figures) who provides guidance, gifts, or confidence. The mentor embodies wisdom or represents the hero's potential self.]
   - Who mentors the hero?
   - What do they offer (advice, tools, confidence)?
   - What is the mentor's relationship to the hero's transformation?

5. **Crossing the Threshold:** [The hero commits to the adventure and enters a new world. Irreversible. Often marked by a guardian or test that must be passed.]
   - What marks the point of no return?
   - What is left behind?
   - What new world is entered?

6. **Tests, Allies, and Enemies:** [In the new world, the hero is tested. They meet allies who will help them and enemies who oppose them. They learn the new world's rules and their own capabilities.]
   - What tests does the hero face?
   - Who becomes their ally and why?
   - Who opposes them and what do they represent?
   - What does the hero learn about themselves and the world?

7. **Approach to the Inmost Cave:** [The hero prepares for the major ordeal. Often a moment of gathering resources, allies, and courage. The stakes become clear.]
   - What is the hero preparing for?
   - What final preparations are made?
   - What is the inmost cave (the place/situation where the hero will face ultimate challenge)?

8. **The Ordeal:** [The hero faces death or the equivalent—literal death, psychological death, loss of identity, or ultimate failure. The darkest moment. Seems like the hero has lost.]
   - What is the hero's greatest fear?
   - Do they face it or avoid it?
   - What is taken from them (or what do they lose)?
   - Is there hope after this moment?

9. **The Reward (Seizing the Sword):** [The hero survives the ordeal and claims the reward. May be a physical object, knowledge, or internal transformation. Often comes with new understanding.]
   - What does the hero gain by surviving?
   - How has their understanding changed?
   - What new power or knowledge do they possess?

10. **The Road Back:** [The hero returns to the ordinary world, but they're changed. Often pursued by consequences of their actions in the special world. Chase, escape, or final obstacle.]
    - What threatens the hero on the return?
    - Do they want to return to the ordinary world?
    - What unfinished business exists?

11. **The Resurrection:** [A final test where the hero must use everything they've learned. Often occurs in the ordinary world. Hero is tested one last time to prove transformation is real and complete.]
    - What is the final test?
    - Must the hero choose between old and new self?
    - Is the hero truly transformed or reverting?

12. **Return with the Elixir:** [The hero returns home changed, bearing a gift or wisdom. The elixir heals not just the hero but the community/world they return to.]
    - How does the hero share what they've learned?
    - How has the ordinary world changed by the hero's transformation?
    - What legacy does the hero's journey leave?

### Best Suited For

- Fantasy and adventure (archetypal form)
- Coming-of-age narratives
- Superhero origin stories
- Quest narratives
- Stories about transformation and growth
- Audience expecting mythic resonance and archetypal patterns

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Mechanical Application:** Forcing all 12 stages into a story creates artificial structure; some stages may be brief or combined
- **Predictable Hero:** Audiences now recognize the pattern; applying it without subversion feels formulaic
- **Mentor Disappearing:** Hero becomes less interesting when mentor guides every choice; hero must find agency
- **Unearned Transformation:** If the ordeal doesn't truly test or change the hero, return feels hollow
- **Literal Interpretation:** The journey can be internal; the "worlds" can be psychological; the "ordeal" can be emotional rather than physical

## Kishōtenketsu (Four-Act, East Asian Structure)

An ancient narrative structure from East Asian storytelling. Emphasizes development and reconciliation over Western conflict-climax-resolution patterns.

### Template

1. **Ki (起, Introduction):** [Introduce the character, world, and situation. Establish the status quo without dramatic inciting incident. The beginning of development.]
   - What is the initial state?
   - Who is the protagonist and what's their baseline?
   - What world or situation are we exploring?
   - Length: 20-25% of total

2. **Shō (承, Development):** [Develop the initial situation. Expand on character and world. Introduce complications or secondary elements that build naturally from the introduction.]
   - How does the situation develop?
   - What new elements emerge?
   - How do complications arise naturally rather than through dramatic inciting incident?
   - Length: 20-25% of total

3. **Ten (転, Twist/Complication):** [A sudden turn or twist that disrupts the expected development. Not necessarily dramatic conflict, but a significant shift in direction or understanding.]
   - What is unexpected about the development?
   - How does this twist change the story's trajectory?
   - Is this twist dramatic or subtle?
   - Length: 20-30% of total

4. **Ketsu (結, Reconciliation/Resolution):** [Reconcile or resolve the tension created by the twist. Not necessarily a happy ending, but a completion or acceptance. Return to harmony or establish new equilibrium.]
   - How is the twist resolved?
   - What understanding is reached?
   - Is resolution happy, bittersweet, tragic, or ambiguous?
   - What is the new state after reconciliation?
   - Length: 20-30% of total

### Key Differences from Western Three-Act Structure

- **No dramatic inciting incident:** Development arises naturally, not from a shattering event
- **Emphasis on process over conflict:** The journey matters more than the obstacles
- **Twist rather than climax:** A shift in perspective or direction rather than maximum tension and release
- **Reconciliation rather than resolution:** Integration and acceptance rather than victory or defeat
- **Harmony and balance:** The goal is often return to or discovery of equilibrium

### Best Suited For

- Literary and character-driven narratives
- Stories emphasizing internal development over external conflict
- Coming-of-age and reflection narratives
- Philosophical or contemplative stories
- Asian or cross-cultural narratives where this structure feels natural
- Stories where meaning emerges gradually rather than explosively

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Insufficient twist:** If the Ten feels minor or expected, the story feels static
- **Forced Western climax:** Trying to make this structure more dramatically climactic undermines its subtle power
- **Confusing resolution with Western-style closure:** This structure's ending may be open or ambiguous; allowing that is essential
- **Pacing issues:** Without dramatic punctuation, pacing can drag if scenes aren't individually interesting

## Seven-Point Story Structure (Harmon/Wells)

A modern, simplified structure combining elements of three-act structure and the Hero's Journey. Designed for clarity and flexibility.

### Template

1. **Hook:** [Establish the protagonist and their world. Give reason to care. Often includes the extraordinary circumstance or character quality that makes this story worth telling.]
   - Who is the protagonist?
   - What makes them interesting?
   - What's their ordinary state?
   - Length: ~10-15% of total

2. **Plot Turn 1 (Inciting Incident):** [An event that disrupts the protagonist's status quo and launches them toward a goal.]
   - What event changes everything?
   - What does the protagonist now consciously want?
   - What's the Major Dramatic Question raised?
   - Length: Brief; turning point

3. **Pinch Point 1:** [Introduce the antagonistic force. Show what the protagonist is up against. Raise stakes.]
   - What is the opposition?
   - How formidable is the antagonist/obstacle?
   - What threat emerges?
   - Length: ~20% of total

4. **Midpoint:** [A major turning point where stakes escalate or protagonist's understanding shifts. Often a false victory or false defeat.]
   - What changes at the story's center?
   - Does protagonist gain new information or lose ground?
   - How are stakes raised?
   - Length: Brief; turning point

5. **Pinch Point 2:** [Antagonistic force strikes again, often harder than before. Protagonist is pushed to limits. Time/resources dwindling.]
   - How does the opposition intensify?
   - What new threat emerges?
   - How is the protagonist disadvantaged?
   - Length: ~20% of total

6. **Plot Turn 2:** [The protagonist makes a choice or gains insight that allows them to face the final conflict. Often involves accepting help, making a sacrifice, or embracing a truth about themselves.]
   - What does the protagonist finally understand or accept?
   - What choice commits them to the climax?
   - How are they transformed from beginning?
   - Length: Brief; turning point

7. **Resolution:** [The protagonist faces and overcomes (or succumbs to) the antagonistic force. The Major Dramatic Question is answered. New equilibrium established.]
   - How does the protagonist face the final conflict?
   - What is the outcome?
   - How has the world changed?
   - What is the thematic payoff?
   - Length: ~10-15% of total

### Best Suited For

- Any genre (very flexible)
- Short stories and novellas
- Screenplays and television episodes
- Clear, efficient storytelling
- Stories where clarity matters more than complexity

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Weak pinch points:** If antagonistic force isn't genuinely threatening, the story feels low-stakes
- **Passive protagonist:** Plot Turns 2 should show protagonist's active choice; if they're passive, resolution feels unearned
- **Unclear midpoint:** The midpoint should be as significant as Plot Turns; if it's unclear, story momentum lags
- **Resolution rushing:** Even in simple structure, resolution needs space to breathe

## Fichtean Curve

An alternative to three-act structure. Emphasizes rising action from the very first page with multiple crisis points building to climax. Created by Johann Fichte.

### Template

**Key Feature:** No exposition in traditional sense. Rising action begins immediately. Multiple complications and near-climaxes escalate tension progressively.

1. **Inciting Incident:** [Opening establishes character and immediate conflict simultaneously. The story's crisis begins from page one.]
   - What problem confronts the protagonist immediately?
   - Why should we care about this character?
   - Length: First pages set tone

2. **Rising Action - Crisis Point 1:** [First major complication or crisis. Protagonist attempts to resolve. This crisis is significant but not the ultimate climax.]
   - What is the first major obstacle?
   - How does protagonist respond?
   - Does this crisis resolve or escalate?
   - Length: ~15% of total

3. **Rising Action - Crisis Point 2:** [A second, larger complication. More is at stake. Protagonist's efforts are tested further.]
   - What new complication emerges?
   - How does it connect to or escalate from Crisis 1?
   - Is protagonist closer to or further from resolution?
   - Length: ~15% of total

4. **Rising Action - Crisis Point 3:** [A third, even larger complication. Protagonist approaches limits. Tension escalates significantly.]
   - What is the deepest complication yet?
   - How is the protagonist disadvantaged?
   - What new information or threats emerge?
   - Length: ~15% of total

5. **Major Climax:** [The ultimate crisis and turning point. All complications converge. Protagonist faces ultimate test.]
   - What is the ultimate confrontation?
   - Does protagonist overcome or succumb?
   - What is irreversibly changed?
   - Length: ~20% of total

6. **Resolution/Falling Action:** [Brief denouement showing effects of climax and new equilibrium.]
   - What is the new state?
   - How has the world changed?
   - What is the emotional payoff?
   - Length: ~15% of total

### Best Suited For

- Action and thriller narratives
- Stories where tension should be relentless
- Screenplays and visual media (visual medium can show action without dialogue)
- Stories where plot dominates character introspection
- Adventure and suspense narratives

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Exhausting pacing:** Relentless rising action can fatigue readers; brief respites are necessary
- **Repetitive crises:** Each crisis point must feel genuinely different and escalated, not recycled
- **Underdeveloped characters:** Rush of plot can leave characters feeling thin; character moments must exist within action
- **Climax fatigue:** If multiple "climaxes" precede the actual climax, the real climax can feel anticlimactic
- **Unclear stakes:** Why does each crisis matter? Emotional investment must keep pace with physical action

## Miniplot Structure (Minimalism)

A structure for literary fiction and character-driven narratives. Emphasizes subtlety, internal conflict, and open endings. No dramatic inciting incident or clear climax.

### Template

1. **Opening State:** [Establish the protagonist's situation, emotional state, and world. Often subtle dissatisfaction or subtle beauty. No dramatic disruption.]
   - What is the protagonist's baseline?
   - What's missing or unspoken?
   - What is the emotional and psychological landscape?
   - Length: ~15-20% of total

2. **Subtle Disruption:** [A small event, conversation, or realization that creates slight but genuine ripples. Not dramatic, but meaningful.]
   - What small thing shifts the protagonist's awareness?
   - How does it challenge their status quo?
   - Is it internal realization or external event?
   - Length: ~15-20% of total

3. **Internal Processing:** [The protagonist contemplates, worries, reflects on the disruption. Internal conflict dominates. Little external action.]
   - What does the protagonist think and feel?
   - What do they suppress or avoid?
   - How does understanding deepen?
   - Length: ~20-25% of total

4. **Quiet Complication:** [A second subtle development that deepens the internal conflict. Often a relationship moment, memory, or choice point.]
   - What does the complication reveal?
   - How does it change the protagonist's internal landscape?
   - Is understanding shifting?
   - Length: ~15-20% of total

5. **Moment of Awareness:** [A crystallizing moment where the protagonist achieves insight or acceptance. Not an action-driven climax, but a moment of clarity.]
   - What does the protagonist finally understand?
   - Is it revelation or acceptance?
   - How does awareness change perspective?
   - Length: ~10-15% of total

6. **Ambiguous/Open Resolution:** [The story ends not with resolution but with the protagonist in a new state of awareness. What happens next is left to imagination.]
   - What is the new state after awareness?
   - Is it happy, sad, or ambiguous?
   - What remains unresolved or uncertain?
   - How is life different from the beginning?
   - Length: ~10-15% of total

### Best Suited For

- Literary fiction
- Character studies
- Stories emphasizing emotional truth over plot
- Coming-of-age narratives (subtle versions)
- Stories about acceptance, loss, or transformation
- Audiences comfortable with ambiguity and open endings

### Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Confusing subtlety with nothing happening:** Subtle ≠ static. Miniplot requires internal tension and evolution
- **Overexplaining:** Literary fiction thrives on implication; spelling out meaning kills it
- **Excessive interiority:** Pages of introspection without external grounding becomes navel-gazing
- **Anticlimactic pacing:** Without dramatic punctuation, pacing requires careful management; scenes must be individually compelling
- **Unclear stakes:** Why should readers care about these subtle shifts? Emotional stakes must be clear
- **Unearned ambiguity:** Open endings must feel authentic to the story, not confused or abandoned

## Comparative Structural Strengths

| Structure | Strengths | Limitations | Best For |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Three-Act Archplot | Clear, satisfying, audience-tested, flexible | Can feel formulaic, requires clear antagonist | Commercial fiction, most genres |
| Five-Act | Epic scope, multiple threads, classical resonance | Complex to manage, may feel dated | Epic narratives, drama, historical |
| Hero's Journey | Archetypal, emotionally resonant, transformative | Pattern now widely recognized, can feel mythic-forced | Fantasy, adventure, superhero origin |
| Kishōtenketsu | Subtle, natural development, philosophical | Demands reader patience, less dramatic | Literary, philosophical, contemplative |
| Seven-Point | Clear and efficient, flexible, screenwriting-friendly | Simplified may feel reductive | Screenplays, short stories, clarity-first |
| Fichtean Curve | Relentless, exciting, non-stop action | Exhausting if poorly managed, hard on characters | Action, thriller, suspense |
| Miniplot | Literary, honest, emotionally true | No clear audience for open endings, requires skill | Literary fiction, character studies |
