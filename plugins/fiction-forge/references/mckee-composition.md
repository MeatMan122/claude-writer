## 1. COMPOSITION: Unity, Variety, and Pacing

### 1.1 The Rhythm of Tension and Release

Stories must alternate between tension and relaxation. This mirrors the pulse of
daily life itself. A story that maintains constant high tension exhausts the
audience; one that stays flat bores them.

**Pacing principles:**
- Start each act at a base of tension, then rise scene by scene toward the act
  climax.
- After each act climax, insert a counterpointing mood shift at the top of the
  next act to let the audience catch its breath before building again.
- Think of pacing like a long-distance runner: speed up, slow down, speed up
  again, creating cycles that push the reader to the limit of their reserves.
- Each successive act climax must top the previous one in intensity and meaning.
- After the final climax empties out the audience emotionally, provide a brief
  resolution for recovery.

**Checklist: Pacing across acts**
- [ ] Does Act Two open at a lower intensity than the Act One climax?
- [ ] Does each act climax exceed the previous one in stakes and meaning?
- [ ] Are there deliberate "breathing room" moments after major peaks?
- [ ] Does the final climax represent the highest point of tension in the story?
- [ ] Is there a brief resolution after the final climax for emotional decompression?

### 1.2 Rhythm and Tempo

McKee distinguishes two separate concepts:

**Rhythm** = the length of scenes. How long do we stay in one time and place?
- A typical two-hour feature has 40-60 scenes averaging 2-2.5 minutes each.
- Variety in scene length is essential. If every scene runs the same length, the
  story feels monotonous.
- Scenes of major reversal should generally be longer, slower, and more fully
  developed. As McKee notes, "climactic" does not mean short and explosive; it
  means profound change. Give these scenes room to breathe.

**Tempo** = the level of activity within a scene (dialogue speed, action density,
emotional intensity).
- Lovers talking quietly: low tempo. Courtroom argument: high tempo.
- A character staring out a window making a life decision: low tempo but high
  dramatic weight.

**The interaction of rhythm and tempo creates pace:**
- As the story approaches act climaxes, progressively shorten scene lengths
  (telescope rhythm) while increasing activity within scenes (spiral tempo).
- This creates the sensation of acceleration toward the climax.
- Earn the right to pause. If you slow down for every minor moment, the
  audience will not grant you stillness when you truly need it for a major scene.

**Checklist: Rhythm and tempo**
- [ ] Do scene lengths vary throughout the manuscript?
- [ ] Are the longest, most developed scenes reserved for major turning points?
- [ ] Does rhythm telescope (scenes shorten) as each act climax approaches?
- [ ] Does tempo spiral (activity increases) toward climactic moments?
- [ ] Are pauses and slow moments earned through preceding momentum?

### 1.3 French Scenes and Internal Rhythm

Even within a single continuous location, rhythm can be created through what
McKee calls "French Scenes" -- the principle that every entrance or exit of a
principal character fundamentally changes the dynamics and creates, in effect,
a new scene.

**Application:** When writing a long scene in one location, use character entrances
and exits to break it into rhythmic subunits. Each arrival or departure shifts
the power balance and relationship dynamics, giving the audience a sense of
movement even in a contained setting.

McKee illustrates this with TWELVE ANGRY MEN: despite being essentially one
room, director Sidney Lumet staged over eighty French Scenes by choreographing
juror movements into sublocations -- the drinking fountain, the window, corners
of the table.

## 2. EXPRESSING PROGRESSION

A story must feel like it is building -- calling on greater human capacity,
demanding greater willpower, generating greater change at greater jeopardy.
McKee identifies four primary techniques to express this progression.

### 2.1 Social Progression

**Principle:** Widen the impact of character actions into society.

- Begin intimately with a few characters and personal stakes.
- As the story progresses, let consequences ripple outward, touching more lives,
  affecting larger communities, reaching broader social impact.
- This explains why certain professions (lawyers, doctors, politicians,
  scientists) appear so often as protagonists -- their personal crises naturally
  ramify into society.

**Application checklist:**
- [ ] Does the story begin with intimate, personal stakes?
- [ ] Do the consequences of actions expand outward over the course of the story?
- [ ] By the climax, are the stakes affecting more people than at the start?

### 2.2 Personal Progression

**Principle:** Drive actions deeply into the intimate relationships and inner
lives of characters.

- When the setting does not allow for widening social impact, go deep instead.
- Start with conflicts that seem solvable, then hammer the story downward --
  emotionally, psychologically, physically, morally -- toward dark secrets and
  unspoken truths hiding behind public masks.

McKee illustrates this with ORDINARY PEOPLE: what begins as a tension about
communication between mother and son descends to the brink of suicide, exposing
the mother's hatred of her own child.

**Application checklist:**
- [ ] If the story cannot widen socially, does it deepen personally?
- [ ] Do early conflicts appear manageable while later ones expose raw truths?
- [ ] Does the progression strip away characters' public masks?

### 2.3 Symbolic Ascension

**Principle:** Build the story's imagery from the particular to the universal,
from the specific to the archetypal.

- Start with actions, locations, and roles that represent only themselves.
- As the story progresses, let images gather greater and greater meaning until
  characters, settings, and events stand for universal ideas.
- Symbolism must be subliminal. If the audience consciously labels something as
  "symbolic," the effect is destroyed. Symbols must be slipped in quietly and
  gradually.

McKee illustrates this with THE DEER HUNTER: steel workers who hunt and drink
beer gradually take on archetypal dimensions, culminating in the image of hunter
and prey at the top of a mountain -- an archetypal image of profound power.

**Application checklist:**
- [ ] Do recurring images accumulate meaning across the story?
- [ ] Is symbolism delivered gradually and subliminally, never heavy-handed?
- [ ] By the climax, have specific images taken on universal resonance?

### 2.4 Ironic Progression

**Principle:** Turn progression on irony -- the gap between expectation and result,
between appearance and reality.

McKee identifies six patterns of ironic story progression:

1. **He gets what he always wanted... but too late to have it.**
   (OTHELLO: gets the faithful wife he wanted, but only after killing her.)

2. **He is pushed further from his goal... only to discover he has been led
   right to it.**
   (RUTHLESS PEOPLE: kidnapping scheme backfires in ways that benefit everyone.)

3. **He throws away what he later finds is indispensable to his happiness.**
   (MOULIN ROUGE: Toulouse-Lautrec drives away Suzanne, then she leaves forever.)

4. **To reach a goal he unwittingly takes the precise steps necessary to lead
   him away.**
   (TOOTSIE: Michael's perfectionism and disguise alienate the woman he loves.)

5. **The action he takes to destroy something becomes exactly what is needed to
   be destroyed by it.**
   (RAIN: the missionary trying to save a prostitute's soul is consumed by lust.)

6. **He comes into possession of something he is certain will make him
   miserable, does everything to get rid of it... only to discover it is the
   gift of happiness.**
   (BRINGING UP BABY: the paleontologist tries desperately to shed the chaotic
   socialite who turns out to transform his life.)

**The key to ironic progression is certainty and precision.** Characters who think
they know exactly what to do and have a precise plan are the best candidates for
ironic reversal. Life kicks them with: "Not today, my friend."

## 3. TRANSITIONS BETWEEN SCENES

### 3.1 The Third Element

A story without a sense of progression stumbles from scene to scene with little
continuity. Between any two scenes, a third element -- a hinge -- must link them.
This link is found in what the two scenes share in common or hold in opposition.

**Types of transition hinges (by commonality or opposition):**
- **A character trait.** In common: cut from a bratty child to a childish adult.
  In opposition: cut from awkward protagonist to elegant antagonist.
- **An action.** In common: from foreplay to savoring the afterglow. In opposition:
  from chatter to cold silence.
- **An object.** In common: greenhouse interior to woodland exterior. In opposition:
  from the Congo to Antarctica.
- **A word or phrase.** In common: a phrase repeated scene to scene. In opposition:
  from compliment to curse.
- **A quality of light.** In common: shadows at dawn to shade at sunset. In
  opposition: from blue to red.
- **A sound.** In common: waves lapping a shore to a sleeper's breath. In
  opposition: silk on skin to grinding of gears.
- **An idea.** In common: a child's birth to an overture. In opposition: a
  painter's empty canvas to an old man dying.

**Application:** When placing scenes in sequence, always identify the linking
element. If no link exists, the transition will feel jarring and arbitrary.
Almost any two scenes can be linked with an imaginative study.

## 4. CRISIS DESIGN

### 4.1 What Crisis Means

Crisis is the ultimate decision point. It is the moment where the protagonist,
having exhausted all other options through the progressive complications, faces
one last action. No tomorrow. No second chance.

The Crisis is the Obligatory Scene -- the confrontation the audience has been
anticipating since the Inciting Incident, where the protagonist faces the most
focused, powerful forces of antagonism in his existence.

### 4.2 The True Dilemma

**The Crisis must be a true dilemma** -- not a choice between obvious right and
wrong, but one of these:

1. **Irreconcilable goods:** The protagonist must choose between two desirable
   but mutually exclusive outcomes. Choosing one means forever losing the other.

2. **The lesser of two evils:** Both options are negative, and the protagonist
   must choose which suffering to endure.

3. **Both at once:** A situation that combines elements of irreconcilable goods
   and lesser evils simultaneously.

McKee illustrates this with THE EMPIRE STRIKES BACK: Luke faces cascading
dilemmas -- irreconcilable goods (fight Vader using technology vs. the Force),
lesser of two evils (attack Vader and risk death vs. flee as a coward), and
finally a lesser-of-two-evils choice (join the dark side vs. take his own life).

**What makes a crisis WEAK:**
- A right/wrong choice with an obvious answer (no real dilemma)
- A choice that can be taken back or revised later (reversible)
- A choice made without sufficient pressure (low stakes)
- A choice where the audience does not understand what is at stake

**Checklist: Crisis quality**
- [ ] Is the crisis a genuine dilemma with no clearly "right" answer?
- [ ] Are both options meaningful -- either both appealing or both terrible?
- [ ] Has the protagonist exhausted all other options before reaching this point?
- [ ] Is this the point of maximum pressure in the story?
- [ ] Does the choice reveal the protagonist's deepest character?
- [ ] Does the audience understand the full weight of both options?

### 4.3 The Crisis Decision Must Be Static

The crisis moment itself should be deliberately still. Do not rush past it or
bury it in action. The audience wants to suffer with the protagonist through
the pain of the dilemma. Freeze this moment. Let it breathe.

As McKee notes, the emotional momentum has been building to this point, and the
crisis dams the flow. Tension builds as the protagonist agonizes, and then the
decision releases compressed energy that explodes into the climax.

### 4.4 Placement of the Crisis

**The location of the crisis is determined by the length of climactic action.**

- If the climax is a single brief action, crisis and climax occur in the same
  scene near the end (THELMA AND LOUISE: they decide, then immediately drive
  into the canyon).
- If the climax requires extended action, the crisis can come at the end of the
  penultimate act, with the entire final act as climactic action (CASABLANCA:
  Rick's selfless decision at the Act Two climax triggers all of Act Three).
- In rare cases, the crisis decision is the Inciting Incident itself, and the
  entire film becomes climactic action (JAMES BOND films: Bond takes the
  assignment, then the whole story is pursuit).

**Risk warning:** Placing the crisis at the Inciting Incident risks repetitiveness.
The entire story becomes one long progression of similar action without the
building toward a decisive moment.

## 5. CLIMAX DESIGN

### 5.1 What Makes a Climax

The Story Climax is the crowning Major Reversal. It must be:

- **Full of meaning** -- a revolution in values from positive to negative or
  negative to positive (with or without irony).
- **At maximum charge** -- the most intense value swing in the story.
- **Absolute and irreversible** -- the change cannot be undone.
- **Self-evident** -- the action creating the change must be "pure," clear, and
  requiring no explanation. Dialogue or narration to spell it out makes it
  boring and redundant.

As McKee puts it, "Meaning Produces Emotion." Not money, not spectacle, not
special effects -- meaning.

**What makes a climax WEAK:**
- Passive climax: things happen to the protagonist rather than through their
  decisive action.
- Reversible climax: the change could theoretically be undone.
- Explained climax: dialogue or narration has to interpret the meaning.
- Insufficient climax: the value change does not exceed all previous reversals.
- Coincidental climax: random chance rather than character choice drives the
  outcome.

### 5.2 Working Backward from the Climax

Once you have the climax, rewrite backward. Every image, beat, action, and line
of dialogue must relate to or set up the climax. All scenes must be
thematically or structurally justified in the light of the climax.

**The ruthless test:** If a scene can be cut without disturbing the impact of the
ending, it must be cut.

### 5.3 Subplot Climaxes

If logic allows, climax subplots within the Central Plot's climax -- one final
action by the protagonist that settles everything simultaneously.

McKee illustrates this with CASABLANCA: Rick puts Laszlo and Ilsa on the plane,
settling the Love Story, converting Captain Renault to patriotism, killing Major
Strasser -- one action resolves multiple storylines.

If this multiplying effect is impossible, climax the least important subplots
earliest, then build toward the Central Plot's climax.

### 5.4 The Audience Wants Emotional Satisfaction

The audience wants emotional satisfaction -- a climax that fulfills the
anticipation built from the beginning. The writer implicitly promises the
audience an emotional experience ("expect an up-ending," "expect a down-ending,"
"expect irony") and must deliver on that promise, though not in the way the
audience expects.

**The climax must be both inevitable and unexpected.** Looking backward, it should
seem like the only possible path. But it must arrive with a rush of unexpected
insight -- a Turning Point within the climax itself.

### 5.5 The Key Image

The climax should create a Key Image -- a single image that sums up and
concentrates all meaning and emotion. Like the coda of a symphony, it echoes
and resonates with everything that has gone before. When remembered, the whole
story comes back with a jolt.

## 6. RESOLUTION

Resolution is any material left after the climax. It has three possible uses:

### 6.1 Resolve Open Subplots

If the climax of the Central Plot does not also close a subplot, a brief
Resolution scene can tie it off. To avoid feeling anticlimactic, use a comic
false twist or surprise to yoke the resolution back to the body of the story
and hold tension to the very end.

### 6.2 Show the Spread of Climactic Effects

If the story has widened into society, the audience has come to know many
supporting characters. A Resolution can show how the climactic change spreads
through their lives -- the birthday party, the reunion, the gathering where the
camera tracks through to show how each life has been altered.

### 6.3 Emotional Decompression (The "Slow Curtain")

Even if neither of the above applies, all stories need a Resolution as a
courtesy to the audience. After a powerful climax, the audience needs a few
moments to catch their breath, gather their thoughts, and leave with dignity.
A brief final image -- a slow tracking shot, a last quiet moment -- provides
this decompression.

**Checklist: Resolution**
- [ ] Are all significant subplots resolved (or deliberately left open for
      thematic reasons)?
- [ ] Does the resolution avoid introducing new conflicts or questions?
- [ ] Is there a moment of emotional decompression after the climax?
- [ ] Is the resolution brief enough to not overstay its welcome?

## 7. THE PRINCIPLE OF ANTAGONISM

### 7.1 The Core Principle

McKee considers this the most important and least understood principle in story
design: **A protagonist and story can only be as intellectually fascinating and
emotionally compelling as the forces of antagonism make them.**

Human nature is conservative -- we never do more than we must. Therefore, only
powerful and complex forces of antagonism will cause a protagonist to become
fully realized, multidimensional, and deeply empathetic. The answer to "how do I
make my protagonist compelling?" lies on the negative side of the story.

### 7.2 The Four Levels of Antagonistic Force

For any positive value at stake in a story, there are four levels of opposing
force, arranged from weakest to strongest:

**Level 1 -- POSITIVE (The Value Itself)**
The starting state or the ideal the protagonist strives toward.

**Level 2 -- CONTRARY**
A somewhat negative situation, but not the full opposite. It is the partial,
moderate form of opposition.

**Level 3 -- CONTRADICTORY**
The direct opposite of the positive value. Fully negative.

**Level 4 -- NEGATION OF THE NEGATION**
A compound negative that is qualitatively worse than the contradictory. It is
the darkest expression of the negative force -- the absolute limit of human
experience for that value. It often involves a perverse form of the positive
masquerading within the negative.

### 7.3 Value Declension Examples

Use these as templates when mapping your story's value progressions:

| Value     | Positive       | Contrary          | Contradictory | Negation of Negation         |
|-----------|----------------|--------------------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| Justice   | Justice        | Unfairness         | Injustice     | Tyranny ("might makes right") |
| Love      | Love           | Indifference       | Hate          | Self-hate                     |
| Love (v2) | Love           | Indifference       | Hate          | Hatred masquerading as love   |
| Truth     | Truth          | White lies/half-truths | Lies      | Self-deception                |
| Life      | Consciousness  | Unconsciousness    | Death         | Damnation                     |
| Wealth    | Rich           | Middle-class       | Poor/suffering| Rich but suffering like poor  |
| Communication | Communication | Alienation     | Isolation     | Insanity                      |
| Freedom   | Freedom        | Restraint          | Slavery       | Slavery perceived as freedom  |
| Courage   | Bravery        | Fear               | Cowardice     | Cowardice perceived as courage|
| Loyalty   | Loyalty        | Split allegiance   | Betrayal      | Self-betrayal                 |
| Maturity  | Maturity       | Childishness       | Immaturity    | Immaturity perceived as maturity |
| Success   | Success        | Compromise         | Failure       | Selling out                   |
| Wisdom    | Wisdom         | Ignorance          | Stupidity     | Stupidity perceived as intelligence |

### 7.4 Why the Negation of the Negation Matters

The Negation of the Negation is what separates good stories from great ones.

- If a story stops at the Contradictory value (love/hate, justice/injustice),
  it echoes the hundreds of mediocre stories produced every year.
- Stories that reach the Negation of the Negation touch the limit of human
  experience and achieve sublimity.
- As McKee states: "All other factors of talent, craft, and knowledge being
  equal, greatness is found in the writer's treatment of the negative side."
- The Negation of the Negation is often the most disturbing because it involves
  a perversion -- the negative force wearing the mask of the positive (cowardice
  that looks like courage, slavery that feels like freedom, hatred disguised
  as love).

**Checklist: Antagonism depth**
- [ ] Have you identified the primary value at stake in your story?
- [ ] Have you mapped all four levels of the value declension?
- [ ] Does your story progress through the Contrary and Contradictory?
- [ ] Does it reach (or at least touch) the Negation of the Negation?
- [ ] Is the Negation of the Negation qualitatively different, not just
      quantitatively worse?
- [ ] Do the forces of antagonism feel overwhelming enough to fully test the
      protagonist?

### 7.5 Progression Through the Levels

Generally, stories progress from the Positive to the Contrary in Act One, to
the Contradictory in later acts, and finally to the Negation of the Negation in
the last act -- either ending tragically or going back to the Positive with a
profound difference.

McKee illustrates this with CASABLANCA: it opens at the Negation of the
Negation with Rick living in fascist tyranny, suffering self-hatred and
self-deception, then works to a positive climax for all three values. The key
is that the end of the line must be reached.

### 7.6 Diagnosing Weak Stories with Antagonism

When a story feels weak, the inevitable cause is that forces of antagonism are
weak. The diagnostic process:

1. Identify the positive values at stake and their progression.
2. Ask: Which value is preeminent and turns the Story Climax?
3. Ask: Do the forces of antagonism explore all shades of negativity?
4. Ask: Do they reach the power of the Negation of the Negation at some point?
5. Rather than trying to invent likable protagonist traits, build the negative
   side -- the positive dimensions will emerge naturally as a chain reaction.

## 8. EXPOSITION: Delivering Backstory Through Conflict

### 8.1 The Cardinal Rule: Show, Don't Tell

Exposition is the facts about setting, biography, and characterization that the
audience needs to follow the story. Skill in exposition means making it
invisible -- the audience absorbs what it needs effortlessly.

**Dramatized exposition serves two ends, in this priority order:**
1. **First:** further the immediate conflict.
2. **Second:** convey information.

Anxious novice writers reverse this order, putting expositional duty ahead of
dramatic necessity. This produces dead scenes.

### 8.2 Convert Exposition to Ammunition

McKee's mnemonic principle: Characters know their world, their history, each
other, and themselves. Let them use what they know as ammunition in their
struggle to get what they want.

**Instead of:** Jack says, "Harry, how the hell long have we known each other?
About twenty years? Ever since we were at college together."

**Convert to ammunition:** Jack, reacting to Harry's bloodshot eyes, says,
"Harry, look at you. The same hippie haircut, still stoned by noon, the same
juvenile stunts that got you kicked out of school twenty years ago. Are you ever
gonna wake up and smell the coffee?"

The audience picks up "twenty years" and "school" indirectly while the scene
drives forward through conflict.

### 8.3 Exposition Pacing Principles

- **Withhold, don't dump.** You do not keep the audience's interest by giving
  information. You keep it by withholding information, except that which is
  absolutely necessary for comprehension.
- **Least important first, most important last.** Pace exposition in a progressive
  pattern: trivial facts early, critical facts later, the most devastating
  revelations saved for the climaxes.
- **Save the best for last.** The critical pieces of exposition are secrets --
  painful truths characters do not want known. Reveal these only when the
  pressure of the story forces them out, ideally at turning points.
- **Never pass on exposition unless the missing fact would cause confusion.**
  If the audience can reasonably assume or infer something, do not state it.

### 8.4 Avoid "California Scenes"

McKee warns against "California scenes" -- scenes where two characters who barely
know each other sit down and immediately begin an intimate discussion of deep,
dark secrets. This feels false because in life, people do not share their
deepest truths with strangers voluntarily.

The rule: revelations of deep secrets must be forced out by the pressure of the
story. The wise writer saves these for the end of the line, where characters
have no choice but to speak the truth. Whatever is said hides what cannot be
said.

### 8.5 Backstory as Turning Points

Powerful revelations come from the backstory -- previous significant events in
the lives of characters that the writer can reveal at critical moments to create
Turning Points.

**The principle:** Backstory revelations should be saved to create explosive
Turning Points that open gaps between expectation and result and deliver rushes
of insight.

McKee illustrates this with CHINATOWN ("She's my sister and my daughter") and
THE EMPIRE STRIKES BACK ("I'm your father") -- both are exposition from backstory
saved to create the greatest possible effect at climactic moments.

**With few exceptions, scenes cannot be turned on nothing but action. We
inevitably need a mix of action and revelation,** and the most powerful
revelations are often reserved for major Turning Points and act climaxes.

### 8.6 Flashbacks

Flashbacks are simply another form of exposition. They work well or badly based
on the same principles:

**Rule 1: Dramatize flashbacks.** Do not flash back to flat scenes in the past.
Interpolate a minidrama with its own Inciting Incident, progressions, and
Turning Point. A well-done flashback actually accelerates pace rather than
slowing it.

**Rule 2: Do not bring in a flashback until you have created in the audience the
need and desire to know.** First establish curiosity by creating gaps and
questions in the present timeline. Only then, when the audience is burning to
know what happened, flash back.

McKee illustrates this with CASABLANCA: the Paris flashback comes when Rick is
drunk and depressed after the Act One Climax. The audience is burning with
curiosity about Rick and Ilsa's past. Only then do the writers flash back.

### 8.7 Dream Sequences and Montage

- **Dream sequences** are usually feeble attempts to disguise exposition in
  Freudian cliches. Avoid them except in rare cases where they serve as
  genuine dramatic events.
- **Montages** (rapidly cut sequences condensing time) are typically lazy
  substitutes for dramatization. They keep the eye busy but rarely dramatize
  exposition effectively.

### 8.8 Voice-Over Narration

Voice-over narration is another exposition delivery vehicle. The test:

**"If I were to strip the voice-over out, would the story still be well told?"**
- If yes, keep it only if it adds nonnarrative counterpoint (wit, irony,
  insights that cannot be conveyed any other way).
- If the story collapses without it, the narration is a crutch covering weak
  dramatization.

Counterpoint narration -- voice-over that adds a layer of meaning that
contradicts or enriches the images -- can be a powerful tool. McKee cites Woody
Allen's films as examples where removing the narration would lose genuine wit
and irony.

Brief telling narration during transitions between acts (as in BARRY LYNDON) is
acceptable. But persistent telling narration throughout a story is a sign of
weak craft.

**Checklist: Exposition quality**
- [ ] Is all exposition dramatized through conflict, not stated in dialogue?
- [ ] Is backstory converted to ammunition characters use in their struggles?
- [ ] Are the most important revelations saved for climactic moments?
- [ ] Is information withheld until the audience needs and desires it?
- [ ] Are there any "California scenes" (forced intimate confessions between
      near-strangers) that should be cut?
- [ ] Do flashbacks have their own internal dramatic structure?
- [ ] Are flashbacks preceded by curiosity-building in the present timeline?
- [ ] If narration is used, does the story stand on its own without it?
- [ ] Does narration add counterpoint value, not just explain the images?

## 9. THE CONTROLLING IDEA

### 9.1 Definition

The Controlling Idea is a single sentence expressing the story's ultimate
meaning. It combines a **value** (the primary value that changes at climax) with
a **cause** (the reason for that change).

The form: **[Value charge] because/when [cause].**

The Controlling Idea is not decided in advance and imposed; it is discovered
through the climax. The meaning of the climax -- whether values end positively
or negatively, and why -- determines the Controlling Idea.

### 9.2 Idealist vs. Pessimist vs. Ironist

- **Up-ending (Idealist):** Positive value wins because of [cause]. The writer
  signals from the beginning that the story will affirm life.
- **Down-ending (Pessimist):** Negative value prevails because of [cause]. The
  writer signals a tragic or cautionary vision.
- **Ironic ending:** Values are mixed -- the protagonist gains something but loses
  something else, or gains what they wanted but at a devastating cost.

The writer implicitly promises an emotional destination from the beginning.
Having pledged a certain emotion, it would be ruinous not to deliver.

### 9.3 The Key Image and the Controlling Idea

The climax should produce a Key Image -- a single visual or dramatic moment that
concentrates the entire Controlling Idea into one resonant image. This is what
Truffaut called "Spectacle and Truth" -- a climax written for the eye (not the
ear) that embodies the story's truth.

## 10. CURIOSITY AND CONCERN: Holding Interest

Two forces hold an audience through a story:

- **Curiosity** (intellectual): The need to answer questions and close open
  patterns. Every Turning Point hooks curiosity: "What happens next? How will
  this turn out?" The answer arrives only at the final climax, keeping the
  audience engaged throughout.

- **Concern** (emotional): The need for the positive values of life -- justice,
  love, truth, courage. The audience identifies with the Center of Good and
  feels emotional investment in the outcome.

**The Center of Good** must be located in the protagonist (at minimum). It does not
mean "niceness" -- it means the audience perceives the protagonist as more good
than the world around them. Even deeply flawed or criminal protagonists work
when the surrounding world is presented as worse.

McKee illustrates this with THE GODFATHER: the Corleones are criminals, but they
have loyalty -- a positive quality absent in rival clans, corrupt police, and
judges. This single positive quality makes them the Center of Good.

**Checklist: Interest**
- [ ] Does each turning point raise new questions to sustain curiosity?
- [ ] Is the major dramatic question ("How will this turn out?") sustained
      until the final climax?
- [ ] Is the Center of Good clearly located in or near the protagonist?
- [ ] Does the audience have emotional reasons (concern) to care about the
      outcome, not just intellectual ones (curiosity)?

## QUICK REFERENCE: Diagnostic Questions

When a draft feels flat, run through these diagnostic questions:

**Pacing:**
1. Does the story alternate between tension and release across acts?
2. Do scene lengths vary, with major scenes getting more room?
3. Does rhythm telescope and tempo spiral toward each act climax?

**Progression:**
4. Does the story widen socially, deepen personally, or ascend symbolically?
5. Are there ironic reversals in the progression?
6. Are transitions between scenes linked by a third element?

**Crisis:**
7. Is the crisis a genuine dilemma (irreconcilable goods or lesser of evils)?
8. Is the crisis moment given stillness and weight?
9. Is it placed correctly relative to the length of climactic action?

**Climax:**
10. Is the climax full of meaning, not just spectacle?
11. Is it absolute and irreversible?
12. Is it self-evident, requiring no explanation?
13. Does it produce a resonant Key Image?

**Antagonism:**
14. Have you mapped the four levels of your story's primary value?
15. Does the story reach the Negation of the Negation?
16. Are the forces of antagonism powerful enough to fully test the protagonist?

**Exposition:**
17. Is all exposition dramatized through conflict?
18. Is backstory converted to ammunition?
19. Are the best revelations saved for turning points and climaxes?
20. Are flashbacks dramatized and preceded by curiosity?
