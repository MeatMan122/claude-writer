## 1. Beat Construction: Action-Reaction Pairs

### Definition
A beat is an exchange of action and reaction in character behavior. It is the smallest building block of a scene. As long as the pattern of action/reaction remains the same, it is one beat. The moment behavior clearly changes on either side, a new beat begins.

### Naming Beats: Subtextual Action

When analyzing or designing beats, name each action and reaction with an active gerund phrase that captures the *subtextual* behavior, not just the surface activity.

- **Do not describe the literal surface action** (e.g., "talking about the weather").
- **Do describe the underlying intent** (e.g., "testing his loyalty" / "deflecting her probe").
- The subtextual action label should indicate both behavior and feeling -- choose gerund phrases with emotive connotations.
- McKee illustrates this in his Casablanca analysis: Rick's surface action of discussing lace with the vendor is labeled "Approaching Her" at the subtextual level; Ilsa's polite disengagement is labeled "Ignoring Him."

### How Beats Build Scenes

Beats accumulate through progressive escalation:

1. Each new beat should **top** the previous one -- demanding more risk, more willpower, more emotional exposure from the characters.
2. The action in each beat should be a **response to the failure of the previous beat** -- the character's first approach didn't work, so they must try something harder.
3. The reaction in each beat should **raise the stakes** -- the antagonistic force doesn't simply repeat its resistance but escalates as well.
4. This creates a **progressive pattern** moving toward the scene's Turning Point.

### McKee's Casablanca Example (Beat Progression)

The Casablanca mid-act scene demonstrates ideal beat escalation across 11 beats:
- Beat 1: Approaching Her / Ignoring Him (lightest touch)
- Beat 2: Protecting Her / Rejecting His Advance
- Beat 3: Apologizing / Rejecting Him Again
- Beat 4: Making Excuses / Rejecting Him for the Fourth Time
- Beats escalate through getting his foot in the door, getting down on his knees, guilt-tripping, saying goodbye, calling her a coward, sexually propositioning her...
- Beat 11: Calling Her a Whore / Crushing Him with the News (that she is married)

Each exchange tops the previous one, placing their love at greater risk, demanding more willpower and capacity for cruelty, while maintaining surface composure. The gap opens at Beat 11, the Turning Point.

### Checklist: Beat Quality
- [ ] Each beat contains a clear action and a clear reaction
- [ ] The subtextual action is identifiable (what is the character *really* doing?)
- [ ] Each beat tops the previous one in risk, intensity, or stakes
- [ ] Behavior clearly changes when a new beat begins
- [ ] The beats collectively build a pattern that arcs toward the scene's Turning Point
- [ ] Even beats that seem random serve the scene's overall design

## 2. Text and Subtext

### Core Principle
Nothing is what it seems. Every scene exists on at least two levels simultaneously: the **text** (the sensory surface -- what characters say and do) and the **subtext** (the inner life -- what characters actually think, feel, and want beneath their behavior).

### Why Subtext Is Non-Negotiable

- Human beings are fundamentally insincere -- not necessarily dishonest, but we all wear public masks. We say and do what we think is appropriate, while thinking and feeling something else underneath.
- A scene with no subtext -- where characters say exactly what they mean and feel -- is "on the nose." McKee considers this unactable and dramatically dead.
- Even in first-person narration, soliloquy, or direct address to the audience, subtext is present. Characters lie to themselves as readily as they lie to others.

### Designing Subtext

- For every line of dialogue or action, ask: **What does this character actually want in this moment, underneath what they are saying/doing?**
- Create a gap between the text (surface behavior) and the subtext (true desire/feeling).
- The most powerful scenes have text and subtext in direct **contrast or contradiction** -- the character says one thing but means the opposite, or acts cooperatively while internally working against.

### Practical Method for Writing Subtext

1. **Write the scene on the nose first** -- let characters say exactly what they mean. This draft is for your understanding only.
2. **Identify the subtextual desire** for each character in each beat.
3. **Rewrite the surface** -- replace direct statement with indirect behavior that implies the subtext without stating it. Let characters talk about lace, or cars, or the weather, while the real drama plays underneath.
4. **Test the gap** -- if you removed all dialogue and just watched the behavior, would you sense what the scene is really about? If yes, the subtext is working.

### Checklist: Subtext Quality
- [ ] No character directly states their true feelings or desires ("on the nose" test)
- [ ] There is a visible gap between what characters say/do and what they want/feel
- [ ] The subtext is inferable from behavior -- a perceptive reader can sense what is really happening
- [ ] Even seemingly honest moments have an underlying layer (characters lie to themselves too)
- [ ] The subtext drives the scene's drama more than the surface action does
