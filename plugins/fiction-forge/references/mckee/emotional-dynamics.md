# McKee: Emotional Dynamics & Diminishing Returns
<!-- Source: Robert McKee, "Story" — Chapter 10. How value changes create emotion and the law of alternation. -->

---

## Two Core Emotions
At the most fundamental level, there are only two emotions in storytelling: **pleasure** and **pain**. Every variation (joy, terror, grief, ecstasy, dread) is a shade of one or the other.

## How Value Changes Create Emotion
The audience experiences emotion when a value transitions:
- Movement from negative to positive creates a **positive emotional experience**
- Movement from positive to negative creates a **negative emotional experience**
- The emotion is produced by the **change itself**, not by the state

## The Law of Diminishing Returns
As McKee frames it: the more often we experience something, the less effect it has.

- You cannot repeat the same emotional beat back-to-back without losing impact.
- Three tragic scenes in a row: the first produces tears, the second produces less, the third may produce laughter (the audience finds the repetition absurd).
- Three comic scenes in a row: the laughs weaken with each one.
- The solution is to **alternate** emotional charges. Follow a dark scene with a lighter one; follow comedy with something that costs the character. This creates dynamic contrast.

## Exception to the Law
If the contrast between emotional charges is great enough, a story can move from positive to positive or negative to negative without losing power -- but only if the second charge retroactively transforms the meaning of the first.

## Mood vs. Emotion
- **Emotion** is the experience produced by a value change. It is short-term, peaks and burns.
- **Feeling** (or mood) is the long-term, pervasive background state. It colors everything.
- The arc of the scene determines the basic emotion (positive or negative). The mood (comedy vs. thriller, warm vs. cold) makes that emotion specific.
- Mood will not substitute for emotion. A scene drenched in beautiful atmosphere but lacking a value change is dramatically inert.

## Checklist: Emotional Design
- [ ] The scene produces a specific emotional response through value change (not just atmosphere)
- [ ] The emotional charge of this scene contrasts with the previous scene (avoids diminishing returns)
- [ ] Mood supports and specifies the emotion but does not replace it
- [ ] Repeated emotional tones are broken up by contrast scenes
- [ ] The emotional arc of the scene matches the value change trajectory
