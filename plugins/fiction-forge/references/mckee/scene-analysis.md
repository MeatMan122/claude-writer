# McKee: Five-Step Scene Analysis Method
<!-- Source: Robert McKee, "Story" — Chapter 11. A diagnostic technique for evaluating whether a scene works. -->

---

## Purpose
McKee provides a precise technique for diagnosing whether a scene works. Use this method to evaluate drafted scenes during revision or audit.

## Step One: Define Conflict

- Identify who drives the scene -- which character (or force) initiates and motivates the action.
- Ask: **What does the driving character want?** Phrase the desire as an infinitive ("to do this..." or "to get that...").
- Identify the source of antagonism. Ask: **What does the antagonistic force want?** Also phrase as an infinitive ("Not to do that..." or "To get this instead...").
- Compare the two infinitive phrases. In a well-written scene, these desires are in **direct conflict** -- not tangential, not parallel, but head-on opposition.

## Step Two: Note Opening Value

- Identify the value at stake in the scene (love, freedom, truth, honor, survival, etc.).
- Determine its charge at the scene's opening: **positive or negative**.
- Be specific. For example: "Freedom -- at the negative. The protagonist is imprisoned by his own obsessive ambition."

## Step Three: Break the Scene into Beats

- Trace each action/reaction exchange through the scene.
- Label each action and reaction with an **active subtextual gerund phrase** (e.g., "Groveling at her feet" / "Ignoring the plea").
- A new beat begins only when behavior **clearly changes** on one or both sides. Repeating the same pattern (even with different words) is still the same beat.
- Number the beats sequentially.

## Step Four: Note Closing Value and Compare

- At the scene's end, examine the value-charged condition of the character's situation.
- Describe it in positive/negative terms, as you did in Step Two.
- **Compare opening and closing values.** If they are the same, the scene is flat -- nothing happened. The scene likely needs to be cut or rewritten.
- If the values have changed, the scene has turned and is structurally functional.

## Step Five: Survey the Beats and Locate the Turning Point

- List all beat action/reaction pairs in order.
- Look for the overall pattern: do the beats escalate progressively? Do they build in risk and intensity?
- Identify the moment when the major gap opens -- where expectation and result diverge most dramatically, turning the scene to its changed end values.
- This is the scene's **Turning Point**. It should be the most powerful beat in the scene.

## Common Scene Flaws This Method Reveals

| Flaw | Symptom | Fix |
|------|---------|-----|
| No conflict | Desires are not opposed; characters want compatible things | Redesign so desires are in direct opposition |
| Flat scene | Opening and closing values are the same | Cut the scene or redesign so a value actually turns |
| Repetitive beats | Characters repeat the same exchange multiple times | Ensure each beat tops the previous; escalate |
| Missing turning point | No clear gap between expectation and result | Design a moment where the world defies the character's expectation |
| On the nose | Subtext matches text; characters say what they mean | Create a gap between what characters say/do and what they actually want/feel |
| Antiprogressive | Beats do not escalate in risk or intensity | Reorder or redesign beats so each demands more from the characters |
| Lopsided | Turning Point comes too early or too late | Rebalance so the turn arrives at the scene's peak moment |
