## Expressing Progression

A story must feel like it is building -- calling on greater human capacity,
demanding greater willpower, generating greater change at greater jeopardy.
McKee identifies four primary techniques to express this progression.

### 1. Social Progression

**Principle:** Widen the impact of character actions into society.

- Begin intimately with a few characters and personal stakes.
- As the story progresses, let consequences ripple outward, touching more lives,
  affecting larger communities, reaching broader social impact.

**Application checklist:**
- [ ] Does the story begin with intimate, personal stakes?
- [ ] Do the consequences of actions expand outward over the course of the story?
- [ ] By the climax, are the stakes affecting more people than at the start?

### 2. Personal Progression

**Principle:** Drive actions deeply into the intimate relationships and inner
lives of characters.

- When the setting does not allow for widening social impact, go deep instead.
- Start with conflicts that seem solvable, then hammer the story downward --
  emotionally, psychologically, physically, morally -- toward dark secrets and
  unspoken truths hiding behind public masks.

McKee illustrates this with ORDINARY PEOPLE: what begins as a tension about
communication between mother and son descends to the brink of suicide, exposing
the mother's hatred of her own child.

**Application checklist:**
- [ ] If the story cannot widen socially, does it deepen personally?
- [ ] Do early conflicts appear manageable while later ones expose raw truths?
- [ ] Does the progression strip away characters' public masks?

### 3. Symbolic Ascension

**Principle:** Build the story's imagery from the particular to the universal,
from the specific to the archetypal.

- Start with actions, locations, and roles that represent only themselves.
- As the story progresses, let images gather greater and greater meaning until
  characters, settings, and events stand for universal ideas.
- Symbolism must be subliminal. If the audience consciously labels something as
  "symbolic," the effect is destroyed.

**Application checklist:**
- [ ] Do recurring images accumulate meaning across the story?
- [ ] Is symbolism delivered gradually and subliminally, never heavy-handed?
- [ ] By the climax, have specific images taken on universal resonance?

### 4. Ironic Progression

**Principle:** Turn progression on irony -- the gap between expectation and result,
between appearance and reality.

McKee identifies six patterns of ironic story progression:

1. **He gets what he always wanted... but too late to have it.**
2. **He is pushed further from his goal... only to discover he has been led right to it.**
3. **He throws away what he later finds is indispensable to his happiness.**
4. **To reach a goal he unwittingly takes the precise steps necessary to lead him away.**
5. **The action he takes to destroy something becomes exactly what is needed to be destroyed by it.**
6. **He comes into possession of something he is certain will make him miserable, does everything to get rid of it... only to discover it is the gift of happiness.**

**The key to ironic progression is certainty and precision.** Characters who think
they know exactly what to do and have a precise plan are the best candidates for
ironic reversal. Life kicks them with: "Not today, my friend."

## Transitions Between Scenes: The Third Element

A story without a sense of progression stumbles from scene to scene with little
continuity. Between any two scenes, a third element -- a hinge -- must link them.
This link is found in what the two scenes share in common or hold in opposition.

**Types of transition hinges (by commonality or opposition):**
- **A character trait.** In common: cut from a bratty child to a childish adult.
  In opposition: cut from awkward protagonist to elegant antagonist.
- **An action.** In common: from foreplay to savoring the afterglow. In opposition:
  from chatter to cold silence.
- **An object.** In common: greenhouse interior to woodland exterior. In opposition:
  from the Congo to Antarctica.
- **A word or phrase.** In common: a phrase repeated scene to scene. In opposition:
  from compliment to curse.
- **A quality of light.** In common: shadows at dawn to shade at sunset. In
  opposition: from blue to red.
- **A sound.** In common: waves lapping a shore to a sleeper's breath. In
  opposition: silk on skin to grinding of gears.
- **An idea.** In common: a child's birth to an overture. In opposition: a
  painter's empty canvas to an old man dying.

**Application:** When placing scenes in sequence, always identify the linking
element. If no link exists, the transition will feel jarring and arbitrary.
