# McKee: Exposition — Delivering Backstory Through Conflict
<!-- Source: Robert McKee, "Story" — Chapter 15. How to make exposition invisible. -->

---

## The Cardinal Rule: Show, Don't Tell

Exposition is the facts about setting, biography, and characterization that the
audience needs to follow the story. Skill in exposition means making it
invisible -- the audience absorbs what it needs effortlessly.

**Dramatized exposition serves two ends, in this priority order:**
1. **First:** further the immediate conflict.
2. **Second:** convey information.

Anxious novice writers reverse this order, putting expositional duty ahead of
dramatic necessity. This produces dead scenes.

## Convert Exposition to Ammunition

McKee's mnemonic principle: Characters know their world, their history, each
other, and themselves. Let them use what they know as ammunition in their
struggle to get what they want.

**Instead of:** Jack says, "Harry, how the hell long have we known each other?
About twenty years? Ever since we were at college together."

**Convert to ammunition:** Jack, reacting to Harry's bloodshot eyes, says,
"Harry, look at you. The same hippie haircut, still stoned by noon, the same
juvenile stunts that got you kicked out of school twenty years ago. Are you ever
gonna wake up and smell the coffee?"

The audience picks up "twenty years" and "school" indirectly while the scene
drives forward through conflict.

## Exposition Pacing Principles

- **Withhold, don't dump.** You do not keep the audience's interest by giving
  information. You keep it by withholding information, except that which is
  absolutely necessary for comprehension.
- **Least important first, most important last.** Pace exposition in a progressive
  pattern: trivial facts early, critical facts later, the most devastating
  revelations saved for the climaxes.
- **Save the best for last.** The critical pieces of exposition are secrets --
  painful truths characters do not want known. Reveal these only when the
  pressure of the story forces them out, ideally at turning points.
- **Never pass on exposition unless the missing fact would cause confusion.**
  If the audience can reasonably assume or infer something, do not state it.

## Avoid "California Scenes"

McKee warns against "California scenes" -- scenes where two characters who barely
know each other sit down and immediately begin an intimate discussion of deep,
dark secrets. This feels false because in life, people do not share their
deepest truths with strangers voluntarily.

The rule: revelations of deep secrets must be forced out by the pressure of the
story. Whatever is said hides what cannot be said.

## Backstory as Turning Points

Powerful revelations come from the backstory -- previous significant events in
the lives of characters that the writer can reveal at critical moments to create
Turning Points.

**The principle:** Backstory revelations should be saved to create explosive
Turning Points that open gaps between expectation and result and deliver rushes
of insight.

McKee illustrates this with CHINATOWN ("She's my sister and my daughter") and
THE EMPIRE STRIKES BACK ("I'm your father") -- both are exposition from backstory
saved to create the greatest possible effect at climactic moments.

**With few exceptions, scenes cannot be turned on nothing but action. We
inevitably need a mix of action and revelation,** and the most powerful
revelations are often reserved for major Turning Points and act climaxes.

## Flashbacks

Flashbacks are simply another form of exposition. They work well or badly based
on the same principles:

**Rule 1: Dramatize flashbacks.** Do not flash back to flat scenes in the past.
Interpolate a minidrama with its own Inciting Incident, progressions, and
Turning Point. A well-done flashback actually accelerates pace rather than
slowing it.

**Rule 2: Do not bring in a flashback until you have created in the audience the
need and desire to know.** First establish curiosity by creating gaps and
questions in the present timeline. Only then, when the audience is burning to
know what happened, flash back.

## Dream Sequences and Montage

- **Dream sequences** are usually feeble attempts to disguise exposition in
  Freudian cliches. Avoid them except in rare cases where they serve as
  genuine dramatic events.
- **Montages** are typically lazy substitutes for dramatization. They keep the
  eye busy but rarely dramatize exposition effectively.

## Voice-Over Narration

The test: **"If I were to strip the voice-over out, would the story still be well told?"**
- If yes, keep it only if it adds nonnarrative counterpoint (wit, irony,
  insights that cannot be conveyed any other way).
- If the story collapses without it, the narration is a crutch covering weak
  dramatization.

Counterpoint narration -- voice-over that adds a layer of meaning that
contradicts or enriches the images -- can be a powerful tool. Brief telling
narration during transitions between acts is acceptable. But persistent telling
narration throughout a story is a sign of weak craft.

**Checklist: Exposition quality**
- [ ] Is all exposition dramatized through conflict, not stated in dialogue?
- [ ] Is backstory converted to ammunition characters use in their struggles?
- [ ] Are the most important revelations saved for climactic moments?
- [ ] Is information withheld until the audience needs and desires it?
- [ ] Are there any "California scenes" (forced intimate confessions between
      near-strangers) that should be cut?
- [ ] Do flashbacks have their own internal dramatic structure?
- [ ] Are flashbacks preceded by curiosity-building in the present timeline?
- [ ] If narration is used, does the story stand on its own without it?
- [ ] Does narration add counterpoint value, not just explain the images?
