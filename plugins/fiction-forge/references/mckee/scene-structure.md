## 1. The Fractal Structure: Beats, Scenes, Sequences, Acts

### Core Principle
Story structure is fractal. The same pattern of turning values through conflict operates at every level of scale. A beat is a micro-scene; a scene is a micro-act; an act is a micro-story. Each unit, from the smallest beat to the full story, follows the same fundamental dynamics.

### The Hierarchy

- **Beat**: The smallest unit of story. A single exchange of action and reaction between characters (or between a character and the world). One behavior shift = one beat.
- **Scene**: A series of beats that turns a value through conflict. Occurs in a continuous or near-continuous time and space. Every scene must turn at least one value.
- **Sequence**: A series of scenes (typically 2-5) that culminates in a greater value change than any individual scene achieves on its own.
- **Act**: A series of sequences building to a climax that produces a major reversal of values, greater than any sequence could achieve alone.
- **Story**: The largest structure. All acts build to the Story Climax, the final irreversible change.

### Applying the Fractal Principle

- When constructing a beat, ask: does this exchange of action/reaction shift a value, however slightly?
- When constructing a scene, ask: does this series of beats produce a meaningful turn from one value charge to another?
- When constructing a sequence, ask: does the cumulative impact of these scenes produce a larger turn than any single scene?
- When constructing an act, ask: does the climax of this act produce a major, irreversible reversal of values?

### Checklist: Fractal Consistency
- [ ] Every beat contains an action and a reaction that shifts behavior
- [ ] Every scene opens at one value charge and closes at a different one
- [ ] Every sequence builds through its scenes to a value turn larger than any single scene
- [ ] Every act climax creates a major reversal bigger than any sequence climax
- [ ] The Story Climax produces the most profound and irreversible change of all

## 2. Turning Points and Value Change

### Core Principle
A Turning Point is the moment when a gap opens between expectation and result, producing surprise, curiosity, and insight. Every scene must contain at least one Turning Point that shifts the value charge of the scene from positive to negative or vice versa.

### What Makes a Turning Point Work

A well-crafted Turning Point delivers three experiences in rapid succession:

1. **Surprise** -- the audience did not see this coming. The world has reacted in a way neither character nor audience foresaw. This jolt of surprise is the initial hook.
2. **Curiosity** -- surprise immediately triggers the question "Why?" The audience rushes backward through the story, scanning everything they've seen, seeking the reason for this unexpected turn. This is a moment of rapid re-evaluation.
3. **Insight** -- the rush of curiosity pays off as the audience finds answers layered into previous scenes. They experience a flash of deeper understanding about character, world, or theme.

### Setups and Payoffs

Turning Points require careful preparation through setups and payoffs:

- **Setup**: Plant information early so that it has one apparent meaning at the time. The audience absorbs it at face value.
- **Payoff**: When the Turning Point arrives, the audience's mind races back and discovers that the setup now has a second, deeper meaning.
- A single setup may have meanings hidden to a third or fourth level, paying off across multiple Turning Points.
- Setups must be firm enough to be remembered but subtle enough not to telegraph the Turning Point.
- Each payoff often becomes a setup for the next Turning Point, creating a chain reaction of deepening meaning.

### Checklist: Turning Point Quality
- [ ] The turn produces genuine surprise (not predictable from what came before)
- [ ] The turn provokes curiosity (the audience wants to know "Why?")
- [ ] The turn delivers insight (a deeper truth becomes visible on reflection)
- [ ] The turn has been set up earlier (planted information takes on new meaning)
- [ ] The turn shifts the scene's value charge (positive to negative or vice versa)
- [ ] The turn opens a new direction for what comes next

## 3. Scene Structure as Miniature Story

### Core Principle
Every well-designed scene functions as a miniature story. It has its own internal arc with the same structural elements as the full narrative.

### Scene-Level Structure Elements

1. **Scene Objective (Desire)**: At least one character enters the scene wanting something specific. Phrase it as an infinitive: "to win her back," "to get the information," "to escape."

2. **Conflict / Antagonism**: Forces oppose the character's scene objective. The source of antagonism should also have a desire, expressible as an infinitive, that directly opposes the protagonist's scene desire.

3. **Progressive Complications (Beats)**: The character's attempts to achieve their objective meet resistance, and each beat escalates the difficulty.

4. **Crisis / Gap**: The major gap opens -- the moment when expectation and result diverge most dramatically.

5. **Climax / Turning Point**: The value turns. The scene reaches its peak moment of change, delivering surprise, curiosity, and insight.

6. **Resolution / New Value State**: The scene settles into its new value charge, which becomes the setup for the next scene.

### The Value-Change Test

For every scene, apply this test:
- **What value is at stake?** (love, freedom, justice, truth, survival, etc.)
- **What is the value's charge at the opening of the scene?** (positive or negative)
- **What is the value's charge at the close of the scene?** (positive or negative)
- **Has the charge changed?** If yes, the scene works structurally. If no, question whether the scene should exist.

### Checklist: Scene as Miniature Story
- [ ] At least one character has a clear scene objective (expressible as an infinitive)
- [ ] An identifiable force opposes that objective
- [ ] Beats escalate progressively -- each harder/riskier than the last
- [ ] A gap opens between what the character expects and what they get
- [ ] A value turns from one charge to another (or to a deeper shade)
- [ ] The scene delivers surprise, curiosity, and/or insight
- [ ] The closing value state is different from the opening value state

## 4. The Nature of Choice and Dilemma

### True Choice Is Dilemma
McKee insists that a choice between good and evil, or right and wrong, is no choice at all -- it is dramatically obvious and trivial. True dramatic choice takes one of two forms:

1. **Irreconcilable Goods**: The character must choose between two desirable things, but circumstances force them to have only one.
2. **The Lesser of Two Evils**: The character must choose between two undesirable things. They want neither, but circumstances force them to pick one.

### Triangular Design
- A two-sided conflict (A vs. B) produces vacillation, not drama.
- Adding a third element (C) creates a triangle of at least nine possible relationship states.
- Triangular choice brings closure because choosing one side means sacrificing another -- a price is paid, making the choice irreversible and meaningful.

## 5. Quick-Reference: Scene Construction Workflow

### Pre-Writing
1. Identify the value at stake in this scene
2. Determine opening value charge (positive/negative)
3. Determine target closing value charge (must differ from opening)
4. Define the driving character's scene objective (infinitive phrase)
5. Define the antagonistic force's desire (opposing infinitive phrase)

### Drafting
6. Write Beat 1: the character's first action toward their objective, and the world's reaction
7. For each subsequent beat: the previous reaction failed or complicated things -- what more difficult action does the character try now?
8. Escalate progressively -- each beat demands more risk, exposure, or commitment
9. Build to the Turning Point: the widest gap between expectation and result
10. Close the scene at the new value charge

### Post-Scene
11. Does the closing value set up the next scene's conflict?
12. Does this scene's emotional charge contrast with the previous scene?
13. Have setups been planted for future payoffs? Have earlier setups been paid off?

## 6. Warning Signs: Scenes That Need Rewriting

- **No value change**: Opening and closing charges are identical. The scene is a non-event.
- **On the nose dialogue**: Characters directly state their feelings, desires, or the theme. Subtext is absent.
- **Repetitive beats**: The same action/reaction pattern cycles without escalation.
- **No gap**: The character acts and gets roughly what they expected. No surprise, no insight.
- **Antiprogressive**: Later beats are less intense or risky than earlier ones.
- **Tangential conflict**: The desires of the opposing forces are not in direct opposition.
- **Mood without substance**: Beautiful atmosphere, evocative description, but no value turns underneath.
- **Missing subtext**: Everything is on the surface.
- **Expository purpose**: The scene exists primarily to deliver information rather than to turn a value through conflict.
- **Lopsided timing**: The Turning Point arrives too early or too late.
