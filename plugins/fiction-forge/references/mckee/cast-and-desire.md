## 1. Cast Design

### Core Principle

The protagonist creates the rest of the cast. All other characters exist because of the relationship they strike to the protagonist and the way each helps delineate the protagonist's complex nature. Imagine a cast as a solar system with the protagonist as the sun, supporting roles as planets, bit players as satellites.

### The Solar System Method

A four-dimensional protagonist needs a cast designed to bring out those dimensions:
- **Character A** provokes the protagonist's sadness and cynicism
- **Character B** brings out the protagonist's witty, hopeful side
- **Character C** inspires loving and courageous emotions
- **Character D** forces the protagonist to cower in fear, then strike out in fury

### Scaling Dimension Across the Cast

- **Protagonist**: Most dimensional (multiple consistent contradictions)
- **Major supporting roles**: Two-dimensional (one consistent contradiction)
- **Minor supporting roles**: May be one-dimensional but should still have one freshly observed trait
- **Bit parts**: Deliberately flat, but not dull. Give each a single vivid detail.

### The Central Question Test

State the story's central question. Each major character should embody a different answer. No two characters should hold the same answer.

### Checklist: Cast Design

- [ ] Does every major character exist in relationship to the protagonist?
- [ ] Does each supporting character draw out a different facet of the protagonist?
- [ ] Is the protagonist the most dimensional character?
- [ ] Does each major character represent a different response to the story's central question?

## 2. Conscious vs. Unconscious Desire

### Core Principle

The protagonist has a conscious desire -- a known need or goal. However, the most memorable characters tend to have not only a conscious but an unconscious desire. The conscious and unconscious desires of a multidimensional protagonist *contradict* each other. What he believes he wants is the antithesis of what he actually but unwittingly wants.

### How This Creates Ironic Structure

- **Conscious desire drives the plot**: What the character openly pursues
- **Unconscious desire drives the character arc**: What they actually need
- **The climax resolves both**: Either the character achieves their conscious desire but realizes it's not what they needed, or they sacrifice their conscious desire to fulfill their unconscious need, or they get neither (tragedy)

### Template: Desire Mapping

```
Character: [Name]
Conscious desire (what they pursue): [External goal]
Unconscious desire (what they need): [Internal need -- contradicts conscious]
How they contradict: [The conscious pursuit actively prevents the unconscious need]
Resolution: [Which desire wins? At what cost?]
```

### Checklist: Desire Structure

- [ ] Does the protagonist have a clear conscious desire the audience can articulate?
- [ ] Does the protagonist have an unconscious desire that contradicts the conscious one?
- [ ] Does the story's structure force these two desires into collision?
- [ ] Does the climax resolve (or deliberately fail to resolve) both desires?

## 3. Antagonism Applied to Character

### Three Levels of Antagonism

Strong stories activate antagonism at all three levels simultaneously:

**Inner antagonism**: The protagonist's own contradictions, fears, and self-deceptions.

**Personal antagonism**: Opposition from other characters who represent incompatible values or desires. These characters should have their own logic, their own story in which they are the protagonist.

**Extra-personal antagonism**: Opposition from society, nature, institutions, circumstance, or fate.

### The Antagonist as Dark Mirror

The most effective antagonists embody the protagonist's unconscious desire, or represent what the protagonist could become if they made different choices. The antagonist is not the opposite of the protagonist -- they are a *distorted reflection*.

## 4. Structure and Character Are the Same Thing

**The function of STRUCTURE** is to provide progressively building pressures that force characters into more and more difficult dilemmas where they must make more and more difficult risk-taking choices, gradually revealing their true natures.

**The function of CHARACTER** is to bring to the story the qualities of characterization necessary to convincingly act out choices.

If you change event design (structure), you change character. If you change deep character, you must reinvent the structure. The two must be developed simultaneously.

## 5. The Audience Bond: Empathy, Not Sympathy

### Core Principle

The audience's emotional involvement is held by empathy. Empathy is not the same as sympathy. Sympathy means likable -- we want them as friends. Empathy means "like me" -- deep within the protagonist, the audience recognizes a shared humanity.

Even the most unsympathetic character can be made empathetic. Macbeth butchers a kindly old King, murders servants, kills his best friend. Yet his guilt-ridden soliloquies let the audience think: "I feel bad when I do bad things too. He's like me."

### Methods for Creating Empathy with Difficult Characters

- Give them a conscience (they feel guilt, doubt, or conflict about their own actions)
- Give them a desire the audience shares (love, freedom, justice, survival)
- Show them as victims of injustice before showing them as perpetrators
- Reveal vulnerability beneath strength, or strength beneath vulnerability

### Checklist: Audience Bond

- [ ] Would the audience understand (not approve of, but understand) why the protagonist makes their key choices?
- [ ] Does the protagonist have at least one quality the audience can recognize in themselves?
- [ ] If the protagonist does terrible things, is there a human reason the audience can grasp?
