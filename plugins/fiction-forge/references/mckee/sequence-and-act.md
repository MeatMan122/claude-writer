# McKee: Sequence and Act Design

---

## 1. The Structural Hierarchy: Beat > Scene > Sequence > Act > Story

Every level of story structure obeys the same principle: a series of smaller units build through escalating conflict to a turning point (climax) at that level, which then becomes one unit in the next level up.

### How the levels nest

- **Beats** (exchanges of action/reaction) build a **scene** that culminates in a scene turning point -- a minor but significant value change.
- **Scenes** (typically 2-5) build a **sequence** that culminates in a sequence climax -- a moderate value change with greater impact than any single scene.
- **Sequences** (typically 2-4) build an **act** that culminates in an act climax -- a major reversal, greater than any sequence climax.
- **Acts** (minimum three for a full-length work) build the **story**, culminating in the Story Climax -- the most irreversible change of all.

### Key principle: escalation at every level

At each level, the climax must produce a change greater in magnitude than the climax of the level below it.

**Checklist -- Structural Escalation:**
- [ ] Does every scene produce a value change (however minor)?
- [ ] Does the sequence climax hit harder than any individual scene within that sequence?
- [ ] Does the act climax produce a bigger reversal than any sequence climax within that act?
- [ ] Does the Story Climax produce the most irreversible, highest-stakes turning point of the entire narrative?

---

## 2. The Law of Conflict

Nothing moves forward in a story except through conflict. Conflict is not merely an aesthetic preference -- it is what carries the audience through time without awareness of time passing.

### Practical implications

- Every scene must contain conflict.
- Conflict held on pause too long causes the audience to disengage.
- Conflict is rooted in scarcity -- there is never enough of anything for characters to have what they want without struggle.

**Checklist -- Conflict Audit:**
- [ ] Does every scene contain active conflict (not just tension or mood)?
- [ ] Is the source of conflict rooted in genuine scarcity or competing desires?
- [ ] If a scene has no overt conflict, is there subtext-level conflict driving the exchange?
- [ ] Are any scenes merely expository or atmospheric without a conflict engine?

---

## 3. Complication vs. Complexity

### Complication (conflict on one level only)

A story is merely "complicated" when all conflict sits on a single level:

| Conflict Level | Genre Examples |
|---|---|
| Extra-personal only (society, environment, physical forces) | Action/Adventure, Farce, Horror |
| Personal only (relationships, family, friends, lovers) | Soap Opera, Domestic Drama |
| Inner only (mind, emotion, body) | Stream of Consciousness, literary prose |

### Complexity (conflict on all three levels simultaneously)

A complex story places the protagonist in conflict on inner, personal, AND extra-personal levels -- often at the same time within a single scene.

### Design guideline

For most fiction, aim for complexity over complication. Keep the cast and world reasonably contained, and concentrate on building conflict across multiple levels simultaneously.

**Checklist -- Complexity Audit:**
- [ ] Does the protagonist face inner conflict?
- [ ] Does the protagonist face personal conflict?
- [ ] Does the protagonist face extra-personal conflict?
- [ ] Are at least two levels active in most major scenes?
- [ ] In key climactic scenes, are all three levels converging?

## 4. Sequence Design

A sequence is a series of scenes (typically 2-5) that builds through escalating conflict to a moderate turning point -- the sequence climax.

### Designing a sequence

1. **Identify the sequence-level objective.** What is the character trying to achieve or resolve across this cluster of scenes?
2. **Plan 2-5 scenes that escalate.** Each scene should attempt a different tactic or face a new obstacle.
3. **Build to the sequence climax.** The final scene should produce a turning point clearly more significant than the minor changes in earlier scenes.
4. **Ensure the sequence climax feeds the act.** The outcome should set up or complicate the next sequence.

**Checklist -- Sequence Health:**
- [ ] Can you state the dramatic question the sequence is answering?
- [ ] Does each scene within the sequence escalate?
- [ ] Is the sequence climax clearly the most impactful moment in the sequence?
- [ ] Does the sequence climax propel the next sequence forward?

## 5. Act Design

An act is a series of sequences that builds to a major turning point -- the act climax.

### The three-act minimum

Three acts is the structural minimum for a full-length work. Two major reversals are not enough to take an audience to the limits of experience. Three create a rhythm that satisfies.

### Act proportions (approximate)

- **Act One:** roughly 25%. Opens with (or builds to) the Inciting Incident.
- **Act Two:** roughly 50% or more. Contains the bulk of progressive complications.
- **Act Three:** the shortest act. Delivers a sense of acceleration toward the Story Climax.

### Solving the long second act

1. **Add subplots.** Weave subplot act climaxes into the middle.
2. **Use a Mid-Act Climax.** Build to a major reversal at the midpoint, effectively splitting Act Two.

**Checklist -- Act Design:**
- [ ] Does each act climax produce a major reversal?
- [ ] Does the first act climax occur roughly 20-25% into the story?
- [ ] Is the second act sustained by either subplots or a midpoint reversal?
- [ ] Is the final act the shortest, creating acceleration?
- [ ] Does each act climax change the story's direction irreversibly?

## 6. Progressive Complications

Every obstacle must be greater than the last in external pressure, internal cost, or both.

### How to build progressive complications

1. **Escalate external stakes.**
2. **Escalate internal cost.**
3. **Never repeat an action type at the same intensity.**
4. **Invent abundantly.** Generate far more scenes than needed, then select the strongest.

**Checklist -- Progressive Complications:**
- [ ] Does each scene's conflict surpass the previous one?
- [ ] Has the protagonist exhausted lesser options before being forced to extremes?
- [ ] Are there any adjacent scenes that repeat the same tactic at the same intensity?
- [ ] Does the escalation feel organic, not manufactured?

---

## 7. Act Rhythm and Value-Charge Alternation

### The core rule

The last two act climaxes must carry opposite charges. If the Story Climax is positive, the Penultimate must be negative (and vice versa).

- **Up-ending:** Penultimate = negative, Story Climax = positive.
- **Down-ending:** Penultimate = positive, Story Climax = negative.
- **Ironic ending:** The Story Climax is both positive and negative. The Penultimate should contradict the overall lean of the irony.

---

## 8. Subplot Design and Integration

### The four subplot relationships

1. **Contradiction** -- adds irony by contradicting the Central Plot's Controlling Idea
2. **Resonance** -- reinforces theme with variation in context or tone
3. **Complication** -- adds difficulty for the protagonist (most important relationship)
4. **Setup** -- opens the storytelling for a late-arriving Central Plot

### Subplot design rules

- A subplot must relate to the Central Plot thematically or structurally.
- Subplots have their own abbreviated act structure.
- Subplot climaxes fill the gaps between Central Plot act climaxes.
- Control emphasis carefully -- if a subplot demands more focus than the Central Plot, reconsider.

**Checklist -- Subplot Integration:**
- [ ] Can you identify each subplot's relationship to the Central Plot?
- [ ] Does every subplot connect thematically or structurally?
- [ ] Do subplot climaxes sustain momentum in the middle?
- [ ] Is emphasis properly balanced (Central Plot dominant)?

---

## 9. Summary: Five Non-Negotiable Rules

1. **Nothing moves forward except through conflict.**
2. **Escalation is mandatory at every level.**
3. **Act climaxes must alternate in value charge.**
4. **Subplots must connect to the Central Plot.**
5. **Complexity over complication.**
