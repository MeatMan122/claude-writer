## 1. Character vs. Characterization

### The Distinction

**Characterization** is the sum of all observable qualities: age, IQ, sex, style of speech, gesture, dress, education, occupation, personality, values, attitudes. This singular assemblage of traits makes each person unique -- but it is not *character*.

**True character** is revealed only through the choices a person makes under pressure. The greater the pressure, the deeper the revelation, the truer the choice to the character's essential nature.

A person's mask -- how they present to the world -- may be utterly different from who they are when it counts. A charming socialite may be ruthless when cornered. A gruff loner may risk everything for a stranger. You cannot know which until you see them choose.

### Why This Matters for Fiction

When characterization and true character match perfectly, the role becomes a list of predictable behaviors. The revelation of deep character in contrast or contradiction to characterization is fundamental to all fine storytelling. Life teaches us this: what *seems* is not what *is*.

### Checklist: Character vs. Characterization

For each major character, define:
- [ ] **The mask**: What observable traits does this character present to the world?
- [ ] **The crack**: What choice under pressure would reveal something beneath that mask?
- [ ] **The gap**: Is there a meaningful distance between who they appear to be and who they truly are?
- [ ] **The pressure**: What specific scenario would force this gap open?

## 2. The Pressure Test

### Core Principle

Pressure is essential. Choices made when nothing is at risk mean little. If a character chooses to tell the truth when telling a lie would gain him nothing, the choice is trivial. But if the same character insists on telling the truth when a lie would save his life, we sense that honesty is at the core of his nature.

### Practical Application: Discovery Through Pressure

Characters are not discovered by filling out profile sheets. They are discovered by designing scenes that force them into dilemmas.

**During ideation**: For each major character, sketch at least one hypothetical dilemma -- a moment where they must choose between two things they value.

**During detailed outlining**: For each major character, ensure at least one scene exists whose primary purpose is to pressure-test the character.

**During writing**: When a character faces a choice, don't default to the obvious option. Ask: what would the most revealing choice be?

### Checklist: Pressure Test

- [ ] Does each major character face at least one genuine dilemma (not a choice between good and bad, but between two goods or two evils)?
- [ ] Does the story contain at least one moment where a character's choice under pressure contradicts their established characterization?
- [ ] Are the dilemmas escalating? Early choices should cost less than late ones.

## 3. Character Arc as Value Change

### Core Principle

The finest writing not only reveals true character but arcs or changes that inner nature, for better or worse, over the course of the telling.

### The Arc Template

```
Character: [Name]
Begins believing: [Value/worldview at story's start]
Key pressures that challenge this belief:
  1. [First pressure -- relatively low cost]
  2. [Escalating pressure -- higher cost]
  3. [Crisis pressure -- maximum cost]
Ends believing: [New value/worldview, or the same value held at a deeper level]
The turning point: [The specific moment the old belief fails them]
```

### Types of Arc

- **Positive arc**: Character grows, gains self-knowledge, becomes more capable or more human
- **Negative arc**: Character degrades, loses humanity, descends into corruption or madness
- **Flat arc with deepening**: Character's core belief is tested and survives, but they now hold it with earned conviction
- **Ironic arc**: Character achieves their conscious desire but loses what they actually needed (or vice versa)

### Checklist: Character Arc

- [ ] Does the protagonist end the story in a different inner state than they began?
- [ ] Is the change earned through escalating choices under escalating pressure?
- [ ] Can you point to the specific moment the old belief/value failed the character?
- [ ] Does the climactic choice complete the arc?

## 4. Dimension Through Contradiction

### Core Principle

"Dimension" is the least understood concept in character. Decorating a protagonist with quirks does not open character and draw empathy. Rather, eccentricities may close him off and keep us at a distance.

**Dimension means contradiction**: either within deep character (guilt-ridden ambition) or between characterization and deep character (a charming thief). These contradictions must be *consistent* -- it doesn't add dimension to portray a guy as nice throughout, then in one scene have him kick a cat.

### How to Create Dimensional Characters

The protagonist must be the most dimensional character in the cast. If a character can be summarized in a single adjective ("brave," "kind," "smart"), they lack dimension.

**Method**: For each major character, identify at least one consistent contradiction:

| Surface trait | Hidden counter-trait | When the counter-trait emerges |
|--------------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| Gentle, soft-spoken | Capable of sudden, decisive violence | When someone threatens those he protects |
| Ruthlessly ambitious | Genuinely guilt-ridden | Alone, late at night, in moments of quiet |
| Intellectually fearless | Emotionally cowardly | When intimacy is offered |

### Diagnostic: The One-Adjective Test

If you can describe a character with a single word and nothing feels lost, the character lacks dimension:
- "Brave" -> Brave in battle, cowardly in love
- "Kind" -> Kind to strangers, cruel to family
- "Smart" -> Intellectually brilliant, emotionally obtuse

### Checklist: Dimension

- [ ] Can each major character be described with a single adjective? If yes, they need a contradiction.
- [ ] Are the contradictions consistent (not random acts that break character)?
- [ ] Does the story contain scenes that bring out both sides of the contradiction?
- [ ] Is the protagonist the most dimensional character in the cast?
