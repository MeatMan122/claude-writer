# Genre Conventions Reference

Detailed conventions for eight major genres. These references help writing agents understand genre expectations, reader assumptions, and common structural/thematic patterns.

---

## Fantasy

Fantasy invites readers to suspend disbelief and accept a world with different rules. The contract: if world-building is internally consistent, readers will believe anything.

### Key Conventions

**World-Building Requirements:**

A functional fantasy world needs:
- **Magic System:** Rules governing how magic works, what it costs, and what it can't do
- **Political Structure:** Who holds power? How do societies govern? What conflicts arise from power distribution?
- **Geography:** Physical layout matters for plot. Isolated locations create different stories than connected ones
- **History:** The past shapes the present. Even simplified history gives texture to the world
- **Cultures:** Different peoples with distinct values, languages, and customs feel authentic
- **Economics:** How do people survive? What resources matter? What creates conflict?
- **Religion/Philosophy:** What do people believe? What values shape behavior?

Depth in one area (magic system) is more convincing than shallow coverage of all areas.

**Magic System Types:**

*Hard Magic (Sanderson's Laws):*
- Magic has clear rules and limitations
- Magic has costs (energy, material, emotional, physical)
- Magic's limitations are plot-relevant
- Readers can anticipate what magic can/cannot do
- Example: Brandon Sanderson's *Mistborn* (Allomancy has specific metals with specific effects)

*Soft Magic (Tolkien's Approach):*
- Magic is mysterious and often inexplicable
- Magic has costs, but they're not always clear
- Magic's limitations are subtle
- Readers sense magic's power through wonder, not rule comprehension
- Example: Tolkien's *Lord of the Rings* (Gandalf's magic is powerful but mysterious)

*Hybrid Magic:*
- Hard magic for certain types (e.g., spell-casting is hard, natural magic is soft)
- Different magic systems for different cultures or characters
- Example: *Harry Potter* (wand magic has rules, but house-elf magic is more mysterious)

**Naming Conventions and Linguistic Consistency:**

- Character names should suggest origin: "Aragorn" feels different from "Bob" or "Wang"
- Place names should follow consistent linguistic patterns
- If you have Elvish names, they should follow Elvish rules consistently
- Inconsistent naming (one character with a Welsh name in a Norse-inspired world) breaks immersion
- Phonetically difficult names may frustrate readers; balance uniqueness with readability

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Info-dumping world-building:** Readers don't need a history textbook in the opening. Sprinkle information through dialogue, observation, and revelation
- **Prophecy clichés:** "The Chosen One destined to defeat the Dark Lord" feels tired without genuine twist or subversion
- **Chosen One Fatigue:** Passive protagonists who are special because they're special, not because of choices or growth
- **Magical Solutions:** Magic that solves problems the plot created (deus ex machina via spell)
- **Inconsistent Magic Rules:** Establishing that magic has costs, then forgetting costs when plot demands it
- **Underdeveloped Secondary Worlds:** Treating secondary worlds as theme parks rather than lived-in places

**Value System:**

- Good: heroism, honor, sacrifice, courage, friendship, justice
- Evil: tyranny, betrayal, cowardice, selfishness
- Often emphasizes epic scope; personal stakes should feel important enough to matter globally

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Often follows Hero's Journey or Three-Act Archplot
- Quests structure many fantasy narratives
- Often features ensemble casts with multiple perspectives
- Climax frequently involves confrontation with dark force/person

## Science Fiction

Science fiction asks "What if?" and explores consequences. The contract: if the speculative premise is consistent with real-world physics and logic, readers will accept it.

### Key Conventions

**Technology Consistency and Speculative Extrapolation:**

- Establish what technology exists and what it can do
- Show limitations and costs of technology
- Technology should create problems as well as solve them
- Future technology should evolve logically from present (not magic in sci-fi clothing)
- Example: If you establish instant communication, you must address why it prevents certain conflicts from escalating

**Hard vs. Soft Sci-Fi:**

*Hard Science Fiction:*
- Grounded in real physics and realistic extrapolation
- Scientific accuracy is paramount
- Explores "what if" based on scientific possibility
- Example: *The Martian* (realistic near-future technology and problem-solving)

*Soft Science Fiction:*
- Less concerned with scientific accuracy
- Focuses on social/political/emotional consequences of speculative premise
- "Science" is scaffolding for human story
- Example: *Blade Runner* (androids and AI are vehicles for exploring humanity)

**World Logic and Internal Consistency:**

- If you establish that FTL travel exists, don't suddenly make distance a plot problem
- If you establish AI sentience, address it philosophically, not as convenient plot device
- Social evolution should follow logically from technological capability
- Example: If longevity treatments exist, how does society structure itself? Who has access? What conflicts arise?

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Technobabble:** Technical jargon that sounds impressive but means nothing. Readers sense fakery
- **Deus Ex Machina Tech:** "They used the quantum flux capacitor to solve the problem" (without prior establishment)
- **Ignoring Consequences:** Showing technology but not exploring impact on society/relationships
- **Inconsistent Future Tech:** Some technology is primitive, some is advanced, with no logical explanation
- **Infodumping Science:** Lengthy explanations of how things work instead of showing through narrative
- **Forgetting Humanity:** So focused on tech-exploring that characters feel like puppets

**Value System:**

- Good: discovery, progress, truth, survival, adaptation, questioning authority
- Evil: ignorance, stagnation, deception, extinction, rigidity
- Often emphasizes cerebral conflict; ideas matter as much as action

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Can use any structure, but often emphasizes theme and ideas
- Climax may be intellectual/philosophical rather than action-driven
- Time scale often epic (future centuries, alien perspectives)

## Mystery/Crime

Mystery invites readers to solve alongside the protagonist. The contract: readers have access to all clues needed to solve the mystery, and the author plays fair.

### Key Conventions

**Fair-Play Rules:**

The classic "Fair-Play Mystery" gives readers everything needed to solve the mystery before the detective reveals the solution.

**Essential Fair-Play Principles:**
- No clues are hidden from readers that aren't hidden from the detective
- Information withheld from readers is clearly identified as withheld (reader knows they don't know)
- The solution must be logically deducible from clues presented
- Supernatural solutions are allowed only if supernatural possibility is established early
- The detective's reasoning must be clear; no secret deductions
- All major clues must be highlighted enough for attentive readers to notice

**Clue Planting and Misdirection:**

*Clue Types:*
- **Physical Clues:** Objects, evidence, traces (fingerprints, blood, weapon)
- **Testimonial Clues:** What characters say (lies, contradictions, omissions)
- **Behavioral Clues:** How characters act, what they avoid, emotional reactions

*Planting Strategy:*
- Plant important clues multiple times in different contexts
- Bury clues among irrelevant details so they're not too obvious
- Plant clues early so readers don't feel tricked by late revelation
- Use dialogue naturally rather than forcing clue exposition

*Misdirection:*
- Red herrings: clues pointing toward wrong suspect (false leads are fair if reader has correct clues too)
- Assumption misdirection: readers assume facts not in evidence (detective doesn't correct them)
- Emotional misdirection: sympathetic character seems guilty (readers invest in believing their innocence)
- Timing misdirection: when clues are revealed affects perception (timing same clue differently changes meaning)

**Red Herrings vs. Genuine Clues:**

*Red Herrings:*
- Point toward wrong suspect or solution
- Should be realistic (not obviously fake)
- Fair-play mysteries allow them because readers have correct clues too
- Too many red herrings frustrate readers (waste time); one or two per mystery is typical

*Genuine Clues:*
- Point toward correct solution
- Should appear multiple times
- May be dismissed by detective or reader initially (but not hidden)
- Become obvious in retrospect

**Revelation Pacing:**

- Solve mystery gradually, not in one massive exposition dump
- Character reactions and minor revelations keep readers engaged between major clues
- Climax should feel inevitable in retrospect, not shocking (readers solved it too)
- Secondary mystery (whodunit) can build tension while primary mystery unfolds

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Hiding clues:** Withholding information readers don't have access to violates fair-play
- **Implausible solution:** "The butler did it" works only if logic supports it
- **Unmotivated crime:** Why did the murderer kill? Motive must be clear and believable
- **Too-clever detective:** Detective reasoning is incomprehensible; readers can't follow
- **Boring investigation:** Repetitive questioning and dead ends without forward progress
- **Late-introduced clue:** Solution depends on information revealed too close to ending

**Value System:**

- Good: truth, justice, intelligence, order, procedure
- Evil: deception, crime, chaos, negligence
- Emphasizes rational problem-solving; emotional truth matters less than logical resolution

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Usually follows Three-Act Archplot or Seven-Point Structure
- Investigation structure: crime → investigation → false leads → revelation → climax
- Often features detective protagonist with sidekick/partner for dialogue exposition

## Thriller/Suspense

Thriller creates tension through action and stakes. The contract: readers know danger is real, and the protagonist might lose.

### Key Conventions

**Pacing Requirements:**

*Chapter Structure:*
- Short chapters that end on hooks or questions
- Quick cuts between scenes (intercutting builds tension)
- Dialogue and action dominate (introspection slows pace)
- Time moves quickly (story covers days/weeks, not months/years)

*Tension Maintenance:*
- Reveal threat early (readers know what danger exists)
- Each scene should raise stakes or reveal new danger
- Temporary victories are followed by larger defeats (escalation)
- Moments of relative calm are brief; danger returns quickly

**Tension Escalation Patterns:**

*Progressive Threat Escalation:*
1. Initial threat introduced; protagonist unaware or disbelieving
2. Threat becomes personal; protagonist directly targeted
3. Threat expands; broader danger emerges
4. Threat seems unstoppable; protagonist's resources exhausted
5. Climactic confrontation at maximum threat level

*Tightening Circle Pattern:*
- Antagonist closes in on protagonist
- Safe spaces become unsafe
- Allies are eliminated or compromised
- Options narrow until confrontation is inevitable

**Stakes Management (Personal → Global):**

*Personal Stakes:*
- Protagonist's life, loved ones, freedom at risk
- Readers care because they're invested in protagonist's survival

*Intermediate Stakes:*
- Community, city, country at risk
- Threat expands beyond protagonist's personal world

*Global Stakes:*
- World, humanity, existence at risk
- Highest possible stakes

**Escalation:**
- Begin with personal stakes (readers invest in protagonist)
- Gradually expand to larger stakes (readers care about implications)
- Balance: larger stakes don't make personal stakes insignificant

**Ticking Clock Mechanics:**

A ticking clock creates artificial urgency:
- Time limit (explosion in 60 minutes, villain strikes at midnight)
- Resource limit (ammunition running out, money depleting)
- Information limit (protagonist must act before being discovered)
- Escalating deadline (each attempt fails; time runs out faster)

*Effective Ticking Clocks:*
- Clear deadline established early
- Readers can visualize countdown
- Deadline affects protagonist's choices (must risk more with less time)
- Deadline consequences are real (if timer hits zero, something irreversible happens)

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Unrealistic Protagonist Competence:** Hero escapes every trap with ease; no sense of real danger
- **Incompetent Antagonist:** Threat isn't credible if antagonist repeatedly fails
- **Predictable Escalation:** Readers see each turn coming; no surprise or shock
- **False Threats:** Stakes keep escalating but feel manufactured, not organic
- **Protagonist Passivity:** Things happen to protagonist rather than protagonist driving action
- **Exhausted Tension:** Relentless pacing without respite becomes numbing; readers stop caring

**Value System:**

- Good: survival, justice, courage, quick thinking, loyalty
- Evil: tyranny, cruelty, deception, ruthlessness
- Emphasizes action and consequence; moral complexity is secondary to survival

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Fichtean Curve works well (rising action with multiple crisis points)
- Fast-paced three-act or seven-point structure
- Climax is action-driven confrontation
- Resolution is brief; action doesn't linger

## Romance

Romance explores intimate relationships and emotional connection. The contract: a central love story is the primary plot, and the ending must be emotionally satisfying to the romantic pairing.

### Key Conventions

**Emotional Beats of Romance Arc:**

1. **Meet:** Introduction and inciting incident for romance. Characters notice each other, attraction begins
2. **Spark:** Building attraction. Chemistry is evident (dialogue, physical reactions, humor)
3. **Barriers:** External or internal obstacles prevent union. Misunderstanding, commitment issues, competing loyalties, social/economic barriers
4. **Intensification:** Attraction deepens despite barriers. Emotional intimacy grows; physical intimacy may progress
5. **Dark Moment:** All seems lost. Barriers seem insurmountable; separation appears inevitable; misunderstanding reaches peak
6. **Climax/Epiphany:** One or both characters realize what they actually feel; commitment becomes clear
7. **Resolution:** Commitment solidified. Future is acknowledged. Emotional payoff is clear

These beats don't require external action; emotional/relational action is sufficient.

**Relationship Arc vs. External Plot:**

*Relationship Arc Dominates:*
- The emotional journey is the plot
- External events serve the relationship (not vice versa)
- Resolution is emotional commitment, not defeated antagonist
- Example: Characters overcoming misunderstanding and emotional barriers

*Relationship Arc + External Plot:*
- Romance and external plot are equally important
- External plot creates obstacles for relationship (danger separates them, duty pulls them apart)
- Both plots must resolve for story satisfaction
- Example: Characters fighting evil together while falling in love

**Heat Levels and Reader Expectations:**

*Sweet/Closed-Door Romance:*
- Minimal physical intimacy; fade to black or no sexual content
- Emotional connection is primary
- Readers expect emotional depth, not physical detail
- Younger audiences or readers preferring non-explicit content

*Warm/Moderate Heat:*
- Physical intimacy is present but not explicit; some detail but not graphic
- Balance of emotional and physical connection
- Readers expect believable progression of physical relationship
- Most contemporary romance

*Hot/Explicit Heat:*
- Detailed sexual content; explicit descriptions of physical intimacy
- Physical and emotional connection equally important
- Readers expect authentic sexuality without shame
- Adult audiences; specific subgenres (erotica, paranormal romance, etc.)

Different heat levels create different reader expectations. Marketing and cover signal heat level to appropriate audience.

**Genre-Specific Tropes (and Subversions):**

*Enemies-to-Lovers:*
- Characters begin in opposition or conflict
- Forced proximity or repeated interaction builds understanding
- Attraction grows as they see past initial judgments
- Power dynamic shift: equals by romance arc end

*Second Chance Romance:*
- Characters were previously involved, separated
- Reunion forces confrontation with past
- Growth and change since separation enables new relationship
- Past hurt must be addressed, not dismissed

*Forced Proximity:*
- Characters thrown together by circumstance (snowed in, traveling, roommates)
- Close quarters force genuine interaction
- Attraction develops through knowledge rather than initial attraction

*Fake Relationship:*
- Characters pretend to be together for external reasons
- Pretense becomes real as genuine feelings develop
- Conflict: when truth emerges, does relationship survive?

*Love Triangle:*
- Protagonist torn between two love interests
- Resolution: choice reveals protagonist's true values
- Can feel overused; must have genuine emotional weight

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Weak External Obstacle:** If barrier to relationship is easily solved, romance feels trivial
- **Unearned Emotional Beats:** Dark moment doesn't feel truly dark; epiphany seems coincidental
- **Consent Issues:** Relationship beginning without clear consent; coercion dressed as passion
- **Flat Rival:** Love triangle where alternate love interest is obviously wrong/inferior
- **Skipped Intimacy Development:** Physical relationship progresses too quickly without emotional foundation
- **Ignoring External Plot:** If external plot is weak, romance feels thin
- **Ending Doubt:** Readers shouldn't question whether couple's commitment is real

**Value System:**

- Good: love, vulnerability, commitment, growth, trust, sacrifice
- Evil: selfishness, betrayal, manipulation, emotional cruelty
- Emphasizes emotional truth and human connection above all

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Emotional three-act structure often clearer than external action structure
- Can use various structures (Hero's Journey, Three-Act, Seven-Point)
- Climax is emotional commitment, not external victory
- Resolution shows couple beginning life together

## Horror

Horror explores primal fear and transgression. The contract: readers will be frightened, disturbed, or unsettled.

### Key Conventions

**Dread Mechanics: Suggestion > Explicit**

The fundamental horror principle: what we imagine is more terrifying than what we see.

*Effective Dread Building:*
- Establish normalcy first (readers need comfortable baseline to contrast fear)
- Introduce wrongness gradually (something's slightly off, then clearly wrong)
- Use suggestion and implication rather than explicit description
- Leave gaps readers fill with imagination (those gaps are where fear lives)
- Delay revelation; anticipation is terrifying

Example: Describing a creature's smell, shadow, or footprints is more frightening than describing the creature itself. Readers' imaginations will create something more horrifying than any description.

**Escalation Patterns: Unease → Dread → Terror → Horror**

*Unease (Slow Burn):*
- Something is wrong, but readers aren't sure what
- Normalcy is disrupted subtly
- Characters dismiss the wrong feeling
- Readers know something bad is coming
- Duration: early story, 20-30% of narrative

*Dread:*
- What's wrong becomes clear
- Threat is identified but not yet confronted
- Characters prepare or flee
- Inevitability of confrontation looms
- Duration: building tension, 30-50% of narrative

*Terror:*
- Confrontation with the horrifying force
- High stakes; survival uncertain
- Maximum action and danger
- Readers are afraid for characters
- Duration: climactic sequence, 10-20% of narrative

*Horror:*
- The emotional aftermath
- Processing what was experienced
- Psychological impact of the horrifying events
- Readers feel disturbed or changed
- Duration: resolution, final images that linger

This escalation is more important than gore. Gore without escalation is just viscera.

**Psychological vs. Supernatural vs. Cosmic Horror:**

*Psychological Horror:*
- The horror comes from human psychology and behavior
- Threat is human villainy, madness, or trauma
- Example: *American Psycho* (serial killer as protagonist)
- Often explores mental illness or human cruelty

*Supernatural Horror:*
- The horror comes from forces beyond natural explanation
- Ghosts, demons, curses, magical forces
- Example: *The Haunting of Hill House* (house itself is haunted/malevolent)
- Often explores violation of natural laws

*Cosmic Horror (Lovecraftian):*
- The horror comes from humanity's insignificance in an vast, uncaring universe
- Incomprehensible forces; knowledge is dangerous
- Example: *At the Mountains of Madness* (ancient beings, forbidden knowledge)
- Often explores loss of sanity through forbidden knowledge

*Blended Horror:*
- Combines multiple horror types for deeper effect
- Example: Supernatural horror with psychological break (character questions reality)

**The Uncanny and Wrongness:**

The uncanny is the familiar made strange. It's deeply unsettling because it violates expectations.

*Uncanny Devices:*
- Dead eyes (character alive but emotionless; wrong)
- Familiar place corrupted (childhood home is sinister; normal place becomes trap)
- Familiar behavior distorted (child speaking with adult voice; recognizable but wrong)
- Impossible physics (doors leading to spaces that shouldn't exist; geometry broken)
- Repetition that shouldn't exist (same person in two places; echoing events)

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Excessive Gore:** Gore can be shocking but doesn't generate dread; it's often less frightening than suggestion
- **Telegraphed Scares:** Reader sees jump scare coming; no fear, just annoyance
- **Inconsistent Threat:** Monster rules keep changing; threat loses credibility
- **Protagonist Stupidity:** Horror requiring protagonist to be idiotic (ignoring warning, separating from group for no reason)
- **Safe Harbor:** Once protagonist reaches safety, no tension (unless safety itself is compromised)
- **Numbing Length:** Horror sustained too long becomes background; readers adapt and stop being afraid
- **Meaningless Death:** Character deaths that don't serve story; readers lose investment

**Value System:**

- Good: sanity, safety, knowledge (in some contexts), community, normalcy
- Evil: madness, chaos, forbidden knowledge (in some contexts), isolation, violation
- Emphasizes transgression and the breakdown of safety

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Slow burn escalation (unease to horror) is more effective than immediate shock
- Can use various structures, but pacing should manage dread carefully
- Climax should be genuine confrontation with horror
- Resolution should address psychological impact, not just physical threat

## Literary Fiction

Literary fiction emphasizes human authenticity, emotional truth, and artistic expression. The contract: readers expect thematic depth, nuanced characters, and prose quality.

### Key Conventions

**Thematic Depth and Symbolism:**

Literary fiction explores ideas through narrative and character.

*Thematic Exploration:*
- Central question or conflict explored throughout (e.g., "What is love?" "Can people change?" "What does loyalty cost?")
- Theme emerges through character choices and consequences, not explicit statement
- Multiple perspectives on theme; no single "right" answer
- Reader is invited to interpret meaning

*Symbolism:*
- Objects, places, or concepts carry symbolic weight
- Symbols are organic (arise from story naturally, not imposed)
- Symbols often have multiple meanings (ambiguity is intentional)
- Symbols reveal rather than explain (they don't state meaning)

Example: A broken mirror might symbolize fractured identity, self-deception, or loss of clarity. The symbol doesn't explain; readers interpret.

**Prose Quality Benchmarks:**

Literary fiction values language itself:
- Precision of word choice (the right word, not the easy word)
- Rhythm and music of sentences
- Metaphors and imagery that illuminate rather than decorate
- Voice (distinctive authorial perspective)
- Economy (no wasted words; every sentence earns its space)

This doesn't mean flowery or ornate. It means considered, intentional language.

**Character Interiority Requirements:**

Literary fiction dwells in characters' internal worlds:
- What do characters think about? What preoccupies them?
- Internal contradictions and hypocrisies
- Emotional responses that feel authentic (not always rational)
- Growth is internal; behavior changes because understanding shifts
- Relationships explored through emotional texture, not plot action

**Ambiguity and Reader Interpretation:**

Literary fiction often resists clear answers:
- Open endings where outcome is unclear
- Multiple interpretations of events/characters are valid
- Moral ambiguity (characters are good and bad simultaneously)
- Readers complete meaning-making themselves

This contrasts with genre fiction (which usually provides closure and clear answers).

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Excessive Introspection:** Pages of internal monologue without external grounding; feels indulgent
- **Obscurity Mistaken for Profundity:** Unclear writing doesn't equal deep thinking; clarity serves truth
- **Prose Over Story:** Beautiful language that serves no narrative purpose
- **Unearned Ambiguity:** Unclear ending that reads as unfinished, not intentionally ambiguous
- **Pretentious Tone:** Language that feels self-conscious and affected rather than authentic
- **Static Character:** Characters think deeply but never change; introspection without growth
- **Plot Dismissal:** Dismissing plot entirely; even literary fiction needs narrative momentum

**Value System:**

- Good: authenticity, insight, growth, empathy, courage, truth-telling
- Evil: self-deception, cruelty, negligence, moral cowardice
- Emphasizes human complexity; villains have valid perspectives

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Miniplot structure works well (subtle disruption, internal processing, quiet resolution)
- Can use any structure, but often emphasizes character arc over plot
- Multiple perspectives/narrators common
- Time may be fragmented (memory, reflection structure narrative)
- Climax is emotional/psychological, not action-driven

## Historical Fiction

Historical fiction grounds narrative in real historical periods and events. The contract: the historical setting is accurate, and the story respects historical reality.

### Key Conventions

**Research Depth Expectations:**

Readers expect historical accuracy in major facts:
- Timeline is accurate (events happen in correct chronological order)
- Historical figures behave consistently with documented character and actions
- Major historical events occur as they historically occurred
- Technology, medicine, and knowledge reflect the period
- Social structures, laws, and customs are accurate

The question isn't "Is this perfectly accurate?" but "Has the author clearly researched and respected the period?"

**Balancing Accuracy with Story:**

Historical fiction isn't history; it's fiction set in historical context.

*When to Prioritize Accuracy:*
- Major historical events and dates
- Documented behavior of historical figures
- Period technology and medicine (don't give characters modern knowledge)
- Social hierarchies and laws of the period

*When Story Can Bend Accuracy:*
- Minor historical figures (create fictionalized versions)
- Invented characters' experiences (extrapolate from known conditions)
- Private moments of historical figures (invent plausibly)
- Small details (which street characters walk; exact dates of minor events)

The rule: don't contradict what's known to serve plot convenience.

**Period-Appropriate Dialogue:**

Period dialogue is tricky. Authentic historical language is often incomprehensible to modern readers; overly modern dialogue breaks immersion.

*Effective Strategies:*
- Use period vocabulary (words available in the time period)
- Simplify sentence structure slightly (archaic grammar is exhausting)
- Avoid modern slang and references
- Use formal address appropriate to the period (titles, "thee/thou" if period-accurate)
- Period-appropriate concerns and values reflected in speech
- Subtext reveals character; explicit emotional expression appropriate to period

Example: Instead of Shakespearean iambic pentameter (incomprehensible), use slightly formal speech with Shakespearean vocabulary available in the 1500s.

**Anachronism Avoidance:**

Anachronisms break immersion. Common mistakes:
- Modern concepts (psychology, feminism, democracy) applied to periods where they didn't exist
- Modern technology (photography, electricity, cars) appearing before invention
- Modern social values as though they're universal (they're not)
- Characters having modern knowledge of historical events
- Modern idiom and reference
- Modern medical understanding applied to pre-scientific period

Small anachronisms might go unnoticed; large ones damage credibility.

**Common Pitfalls:**

- **Info-Dumping History:** Lengthy exposition about the historical period instead of integrating information through narrative
- **Saccharine Romanticism:** Portraying the past as better, simpler, more noble than reality
- **Condescending to the Past:** Characters act stupidly because "they didn't know then"
- **Present-Day Values:** Judging historical figures/customs by modern morality without understanding historical context
- **Invented Historical Figures:** Creating major historical characters when known figures would work better
- **Ignoring Historical Complexity:** Treating the period as monolithic (gender/race/class distinctions within periods)
- **Contrived Historical Accuracy:** Forcing real events or people into story awkwardly to prove research

**Value System:**

- Often reflects period's actual values (not modern values)
- Complexity: characters have conflicting values (period beliefs and character conscience)
- Growth often involves expanding understanding of the world

**Structural Tendencies:**

- Often emphasizes character over plot (personal story within historical context)
- Can use various structures, but pacing often slower (period feel)
- Multiple perspectives common (showing different classes/genders/experiences)
- Often questions "what really happened" through fictional narrative

## Comparative Genre Strengths and Demands

| Genre | Primary Focus | Key Skill | Reader Expectation | Primary Pitfall |
|-------|---------------|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Fantasy | World-building | Internal consistency | Immersive secondary world | Info-dumping or inconsistent magic |
| Sci-Fi | Ideas & speculation | Logical extrapolation | Intellectually coherent premise | Technobabble; ignored consequences |
| Mystery | Problem-solving | Fair clue-planting | Solvable puzzle | Hidden information or implausible solution |
| Thriller | Pacing & tension | Escalation management | Relentless action & stakes | Predictability or unearned tension |
| Romance | Emotional connection | Character authenticity | Satisfying relationship arc | Unearned emotional beats or weak obstacles |
| Horror | Emotional impact | Dread building | Genuine fear or unease | Excessive gore replacing psychology |
| Literary | Human truth | Prose & insight | Thematic depth & authenticity | Obscurity or unearned ambiguity |
| Historical | Authentic setting | Period research | Respectful historical accuracy | Anachronism or forced historical events |

## Cross-Genre Considerations

**Blended Genres:**

Modern fiction often blends genre conventions:
- Historical + Fantasy (secondary world set in inspired-by-history period)
- Mystery + Thriller (solving mystery while being pursued)
- Literary + Science Fiction (sci-fi exploring philosophical ideas)
- Romance + (any genre) (central love story in other genre's context)

When blending genres:
- Clarify which genre dominates
- Honor conventions of both genres
- Don't let one genre undermine the other (romance shouldn't trivialize mystery's clue structure)
- Reader expectations should be clear from beginning
