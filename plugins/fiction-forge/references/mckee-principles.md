# Robert McKee's Story Principles

A comprehensive reference of McKee's narrative theory organized for AI writing agents. These principles form the foundation of story structure analysis and creation.

## The Story Triangle: Three Approaches to Narrative Design

McKee identifies three fundamental design patterns that define how stories are built:

### Archplot (Classical Design)

The dominant form in commercial fiction and film.

**Core Characteristics:**
- **Protagonist:** Active, drives the narrative forward
- **Time:** Linear progression from beginning to end
- **Causality:** Events connected by cause-and-effect; characters make choices that drive consequences
- **Multiple Protagonists:** Typically one central protagonist
- **Reality:** Consistent world with unified internal logic
- **Ending:** Closed ending; all major questions answered
- **Conflict:** External; protagonist struggles against outer antagonistic forces
- **Audience Expectation:** Clear resolution, sense of completion

**When to Use:** Commercial fiction, most genre fiction, screenplays, mainstream narratives where audiences expect clear answers and satisfying conclusions.

### Miniplot (Minimalism)

The foundation of literary and contemporary fiction.

**Core Characteristics:**
- **Protagonist:** Passive or reluctant; shaped by forces rather than driving action
- **Time:** Linear but with internal time (memory, reflection)
- **Causality:** Events connected by correlation and internal response rather than external cause-effect
- **Multiple Protagonists:** Multiple perspectives, ensemble approach possible
- **Reality:** Consistent but inward-focused
- **Ending:** Open ending; ambiguity and unresolved tensions
- **Conflict:** Internal; protagonist struggles within themselves or against social/emotional constraints
- **Audience Expectation:** Insight and resonance; "This is how life actually feels"

**When to Use:** Literary fiction, character studies, contemporary drama, stories where emotional truth matters more than plot resolution.

### Antiplot (Anti-Structure)

Experimental and avant-garde narrative.

**Core Characteristics:**
- **Protagonist:** May be absent or fragmented
- **Time:** Nonlinear; chronology may be deliberately scrambled
- **Causality:** Coincidence dominates; events may lack logical connection
- **Multiple Protagonists:** Fragmented perspectives; identity itself may be questioned
- **Reality:** Inconsistent; multiple realities may coexist
- **Ending:** No ending in conventional sense; story stops rather than concludes
- **Conflict:** May be absent or incomprehensible
- **Audience Expectation:** Provocation and artistic experience; meaning-making is reader's responsibility

**When to Use:** Experimental literature, postmodern narrative, deliberately challenging work, stories where form and convention-breaking is the point.

### The Seven Structural Dimensions

These binary choices determine where a story falls on the triangle:

1. **Protagonist:** Active (drives plot through choice) ↔ Passive (shaped by circumstance)
2. **Time:** Linear (chronological progression) ↔ Nonlinear (chronology disrupted)
3. **Causality:** Causality (cause → effect) ↔ Coincidence (random events)
4. **Protagonists:** Single protagonist ↔ Multiple protagonists
5. **Realities:** Consistent reality (one logical world) ↔ Inconsistent realities (multiple logics coexist)
6. **Ending:** Closed ending (resolution) ↔ Open ending (ambiguity)
7. **Conflict:** External conflict (against world) ↔ Internal conflict (against self)

**Application:** Each story makes choices across these dimensions. Archplot clusters toward Active/Linear/Causality/Single/Consistent/Closed/External. Miniplot clusters toward Passive/Linear/Causality/Multiple/Consistent/Open/Internal. Antiplot clusters toward any combination, with deliberate inconsistency.

## Genre Conventions

Genres are contracts between writer and audience. The audience arrives with specific expectations about character types, situations, emotions, and values. The writer can honor, subvert, or blend conventions—but must do so intentionally and with awareness.

### What Genre Provides

- **Character Types:** Archetypal roles (the hero, the mentor, the trickster, etc.)
- **Setting Conventions:** Where and when stories typically occur
- **Plot Situations:** Stock scenarios the genre audience recognizes and anticipates
- **Value Systems:** What matters morally and emotionally in this world
- **Emotional Tone:** The mood and pacing the audience expects
- **Visual/Sensory Palette:** The texture and atmosphere of the genre world

### Genre-Specific Value Systems and Conventions

#### Action/Adventure
**Value System:** Courage, competence, justice, honor, survival
**Character Types:** Hero, villain, sidekick/ally, mentor, love interest
**Plot Situations:** Chase, rescue, heist, infiltration, final battle
**Pacing:** Fast; high physical stakes; clear good vs. evil
**Common Tropes:** Underestimated hero, last-minute rescue, villain's plan revealed at climax
**Pitfalls:** Cardboard characters, spectacle over stakes, incomprehensible action sequences

#### Comedy
**Value System:** Laughter, truth-telling, social observation, absurdity
**Character Types:** Fool, straight man, antagonist-as-obstacle, unlikely hero
**Plot Situations:** Mistaken identity, escalating misunderstandings, absurd premises taken seriously
**Pacing:** Rapid-fire; timing is everything; escalation of complications
**Common Tropes:** Fish out of water, role reversal, physical comedy, witty banter
**Pitfalls:** Jokes that don't land in text, relying on slapstick without character, meanness disguised as humor

#### Drama
**Value System:** Human truth, emotional authenticity, relationships, growth
**Character Types:** Flawed protagonist, antagonist with valid motives, supporting characters with depth
**Plot Situations:** Relationship crisis, moral dilemma, loss and recovery, self-realization
**Pacing:** Measured; internal beats matter as much as external events
**Common Tropes:** Reunion, confrontation, sacrifice, reconciliation
**Pitfalls:** Melodrama, relentless sadness, navel-gazing without stakes, predictable arcs

#### Horror
**Value System:** Primal fear, transgression, survival, sanity, innocence lost
**Character Types:** Ordinary person in extraordinary terror, monster/antagonist, skeptic turned believer
**Plot Situations:** Haunting, infection/possession, encounter with the unknown, siege, descent
**Pacing:** Escalating dread; slow burn to explosive terror
**Common Tropes:** Jump scares, isolation, countdown to apocalypse, forbidden knowledge
**Pitfalls:** Excessive gore replacing atmosphere, telegraphed scares, inconsistent monster behavior

#### Romance
**Value System:** Love, connection, vulnerability, commitment, emotional truth
**Character Types:** Romantic protagonist (usually dual), love interest, obstacle character, confidant
**Plot Situations:** Meeting, barriers to union, dark moment, commitment
**Pacing:** Balanced between external plot and emotional journey
**Common Tropes:** Enemies-to-lovers, second chance romance, forced proximity, reunion
**Pitfalls:** Weak external plot, unearned emotional beats, consent issues, flat rivals

#### Thriller/Suspense
**Value System:** Tension, stakes, justice, survival, truth
**Character Types:** Protagonist in danger, antagonist with clear motivation, ally/love interest at risk
**Plot Situations:** Cat-and-mouse game, race against time, uncovering conspiracy, survival scenario
**Pacing:** Relentless escalation; chapters end on hooks; short scenes and quick cuts
**Common Tropes:** Ticking clock, MacGuffin, double-cross, safe house compromise
**Pitfalls:** Predictable reveals, coincidental solutions, protagonist stupidity required for plot, loss of tension in resolution

#### Fantasy
**Value System:** Good vs. evil, heroism, magic, wonder, destiny or self-determination
**Character Types:** Chosen one or reluctant hero, wise mentor, dark lord/antagonist, loyal companions
**Plot Situations:** Quest, training montage, betrayal, final battle, claim throne or save world
**Pacing:** Epic; allows for world-building; climax often large-scale
**Common Tropes:** Prophecy, magic system with rules, hidden identity, fellowship
**Pitfalls:** Info-dumping world-building, "chosen one" passivity, magic as plot device with no rules, unpronounceable names with no linguistic consistency

#### Science Fiction
**Value System:** Ideas, technology, consequence, survival, what it means to be human
**Character Types:** Protagonist confronting new reality, antagonist embodying opposing ideology, mentor figure
**Plot Situations:** First contact, technological breakthrough with consequences, dystopian resistance, space exploration
**Pacing:** Allows for conceptual exploration; can be fast or measured depending on subgenre
**Common Tropes:** Fish-out-of-water in future/alien society, technological singularity, time paradox, AI gaining consciousness
**Pitfalls:** Technobabble without meaning, ignoring consequences of technology, inconsistent world logic, deus ex machina solutions

#### Mystery/Crime
**Value System:** Justice, truth, order restored, intelligence, procedure
**Character Types:** Detective/investigator, victim, culprit, red herring suspects, mentor/partner
**Plot Situations:** Crime, investigation, false leads, revelation, justice/truth
**Pacing:** Controlled reveal; holds information back strategically
**Common Tropes:** Brilliant detective, twist ending, hidden connection between suspects, detective's personal case
**Pitfalls:** Unfair clue-hiding, solutions based on information reader doesn't have, inconsistent detective logic, killer revealed out of nowhere

#### Literary Fiction
**Value System:** Human authenticity, insight, beauty, moral complexity, transformation
**Character Types:** Complex protagonists with contradictions, antagonists with valid perspectives, subtle relationships
**Plot Situations:** Coming of age, loss, self-discovery, relationship evolution, confrontation with truth
**Pacing:** Internal pacing; emotional beats dominate; external plot often minimal
**Common Tropes:** Epiphany, memory as structure, unreliable narrator, quiet ending with big meaning
**Pitfalls:** Excessive introspection without action, obscurity mistaken for profundity, prose over story, unearned ambiguity

### The Rule of Genre Conventions

**Know the conventions before you write.** Ignorance of convention often reads as incompetence rather than innovation.

**Decide your relationship to convention:** Will you honor it? Subvert it? Blend conventions from multiple genres? Each choice is valid, but the choice must be deliberate.

**Never break a convention the audience doesn't recognize.** If you violate a genre expectation the audience doesn't yet know exists, they'll accept it. But if you break a convention they recognize, you'd better have a compelling reason and the reader should feel the violation was intentional and meaningful.

**Genre knowledge is not limitation—it's liberation.** Conventions provide scaffolding. Within understood structures, characters and emotions can achieve profound depth.

## The Inciting Incident

The inciting incident is the event that shatters the protagonist's life and launches the story. It is the story's most important structural moment.

### Essential Characteristics

**Timing:** Must occur within the first 25% of the story. Too late and the exposition becomes unbearable.

**Impact:** Must radically upset the protagonist's life balance. A minor event is not an inciting incident. The protagonist's world must be fundamentally altered—they cannot simply walk away and resume their normal life.

**Irreversibility:** The protagonist cannot undo the inciting incident. They might want to restore the old status quo, but the story has made it impossible.

**Germane:** The inciting incident must be relevant to the world and protagonist. It should not feel random or grafted on; it must arise organically from the situation.

**Raises the Major Dramatic Question:** The inciting incident poses the central question the entire story exists to answer. "Will [protagonist] achieve [goal] despite [obstacle]?" Everything else flows from this question.

### Conscious Desire vs. Unconscious Desire

The inciting incident creates two simultaneous desires:

**Conscious Desire:** What the protagonist openly acknowledges they want and pursue. This is what they tell themselves and others they're after. It drives the plot.

**Unconscious Desire:** What the protagonist actually needs but doesn't recognize. This is often the opposite of or complement to their conscious desire. It drives the character arc.

Example: A protagonist consciously desires wealth and power (plot). Unconsciously, they need love and human connection (character arc). The story must satisfy both or create ironic tension between them.

### The Spine: The Through-Line of Story

The spine is the thematic and structural through-line connecting all story elements. It's the core argument or question the story makes:

- In *Hamlet*, the spine might be: "Can a thoughtful man survive in a corrupt world of action?"
- In *Cinderella*, the spine might be: "Can goodness be rewarded despite injustice?"

The spine keeps the narrative coherent and ensures that scenes, characters, and turns serve a unified vision rather than random events strung together.

### Quality Requirements for Inciting Incidents

**Dynamic:** Something must happen. Not a realization or a conversation—an event that cannot be undone.

**Irreversible:** The protagonist cannot restore the old status quo through their own agency (though restoration might come through sacrifice).

**Germane:** Arise organically from character and world. A meteor strike might be irreversible but won't serve the story if the world doesn't support it.

**Raises the Major Dramatic Question:** Make us ask "What will happen next?" and "Will the protagonist succeed?"

### Design Patterns for the Inciting Incident

**By Causality (Protagonist's Choice):**
The protagonist makes a choice that irrevocably changes their life. Example: Luke Skywalker buys the droids.
- Pro: Establishes character agency and motivation
- Con: Requires a protagonist active and reckless enough to cause their own catastrophe

**By Coincidence (Random Event):**
An event happens to the protagonist that they didn't cause or foresee. Example: Elmer Fudd's gun backfires.
- Pro: Allows for passive protagonists; can emphasize fate or bad luck
- Con: Can feel arbitrary unless prepared by prior establishment

**Two-Event Pattern (Setup + Payoff):**
An earlier event sets up a later inciting incident. The setup establishes vulnerability; the payoff exploits it. Example: The father tells the son "Never trust strangers," then the stranger arrives with an irresistible offer.
- Pro: Makes the incident feel more integrated into the story
- Con: Requires longer exposition to establish setup

## Act Design

Stories don't move uniformly. They build and peak and resolve in identifiable patterns.

### The Five-Part Dramatic Structure

**Exposition:** The introduction of character, world, and situation. Establishes the protagonist's ordinary life before disruption.

**Rising Action:** The protagonist confronts obstacles and complications arising from the inciting incident. Each action provokes counteraction. Effort escalates. Stakes rise.

**Climax:** The major dramatic question is answered through irreversible action. The protagonist makes a conscious choice facing ultimate pressure, and that choice transforms the world.

**Falling Action:** The consequences of the climactic action reverberate. Loose ends tie off. Secondary conflicts resolve.

**Resolution:** The new status quo after the climax. Emotional decompression and the thematic payoff.

### Progressive Complications: The Engine of Rising Action

Rising action isn't random accumulation of obstacles. It follows a logic:

**Action/Reaction Cycles:** Each complication provokes a response. Each response creates a new complication. This back-and-forth escalates the conflict.

**Gap Between Expectation and Result:** The protagonist attempts something expecting a certain outcome. The actual result differs, creating surprise and new problems.

**Escalating Effort:** As complications mount, the protagonist must invest more—time, resources, emotional capital, moral compromise—to continue. Eventually they're in so deep they can't turn back.

**Progression:** Each act climax must be more impactful than the previous one. The story must feel like it's building toward something greater, not spinning in circles.

### Act Proportions (Three-Act Structure)

**Act One:** 20-25% of total length
- Exposition + Inciting Incident
- Establish stakes and raise the Major Dramatic Question

**Act Two:** 50-60% of total length
- Rising Action with multiple complications and complications within complications
- Midpoint reversal or false victory
- Escalating stakes

**Act Three:** 15-20% of total length
- Crisis + Climax + Resolution
- Moving quickly; reader expectations heightened
- Emotional payoff

**Note:** Act Two is proportionally largest because rising action is where character is tested most thoroughly. Act Three moves faster because tension is maximized.

### Working Backward from Climax

McKee recommends reverse-engineering your structure: Start with where your story peaks. What is the climactic choice your protagonist must make? What circumstance forces that choice? What prior complications make that moment necessary? Build backward to your inciting incident.

This ensures the climax doesn't feel arbitrary and that earlier complications genuinely lead to the ultimate confrontation.

## Scene Design

A scene is a unit of action in continuous time and space in which characters pursue a desire through interaction, generating conflict.

### The Value Turn

Every scene must *turn a value*—move from positive to negative or vice versa, from hope to despair, from trust to suspicion, from ignorance to knowledge.

Examples of value turns:
- Security → Danger
- Hope → Despair
- Trust → Betrayal
- Ignorance → Knowledge
- Love → Hate
- Courage → Fear
- Victory → Defeat

A scene that doesn't turn a value doesn't advance the story. It might be well-written, but it's expendable. **Cut it.**

### Beats: The Smallest Unit of Action

A beat is an exchange of behavior in action-reaction pairs. Character A does something; Character B reacts; Character A responds to the reaction.

These micro-cycles create the texture of a scene. Beats generate conflict, revelation, and momentum. A scene might contain dozens of beats building toward a larger value turn.

### Scene Architecture: Beats → Sequences → Acts

- **Beats:** Individual action-reaction exchanges (seconds to minutes of story time)
- **Scenes:** Units of continuous time and space with an overall value turn (minutes to hours of story time)
- **Sequences:** 2-5 scenes that build together toward a larger turning point (hours to days of story time)
- **Acts:** Multiple sequences building to an act climax (days to years of story time)

Each level contains the level below it, creating fractal structure. The principles that govern scenes govern sequences and acts.

### Practical Application

Before writing a scene, ask:
- What value does this scene turn?
- What does my protagonist desire in this scene?
- What opposes them?
- What is the result—how does the value shift?

If you can't answer these questions, the scene isn't ready to write.

## Crisis, Climax, and Resolution

These three elements form the story's final movement and its most crucial structural points.

### Crisis: The True Dilemma

The crisis is the moment where the protagonist faces irreconcilable goods or evils. A true dilemma has no "right" answer—both options are unsatisfying.

Examples:
- Save the city or save your child (you cannot do both)
- Tell the truth and destroy your marriage, or lie and preserve it
- Compromise your principles and survive, or hold them and die
- Betray your friend or betray your country

A crisis is not a simple obstacle to overcome. It's a genuine bind where the protagonist must choose between two fundamentally incompatible goods or evils.

**Weak crises** offer one good choice and one bad choice (this isn't a dilemma, it's easy). True crises force the protagonist to sacrifice something important no matter what they choose.

### Climax: Irreversible Action

The climax is the action the protagonist takes in response to the crisis. It is:

- **A conscious choice:** The protagonist decides what to do
- **Taken under maximum pressure:** Options are exhausted; time is running out
- **Irreversible:** The consequences cannot be undone
- **Sufficient:** The action directly causes the absolute change in the story's world

The climax doesn't resolve the crisis perfectly. It resolves it by the protagonist making a choice and living with consequences.

**Weak climaxes** are passive (something happens to the protagonist), reversible (it can be undone), or insufficient (it doesn't actually resolve the story's central question).

### Resolution: The New Status Quo

The resolution shows the effects of the climactic action. It provides emotional decompression and thematic payoff.

In closed-ending structures, the resolution may be extended (showing the full aftermath). In open-ending structures, the resolution may be brief, leaving implications for the reader to imagine.

The resolution must feel earned. If the climax was insufficient, no amount of resolution can rescue the story. But a strong climax without resolution feels incomplete and emotionally unsatisfying.

### Value Reversals: Types and Patterns

The climax typically involves a reversal of the story's central value:

**Positive → Negative Reversal:**
What seemed good transforms. Hope becomes despair. Victory becomes defeat. Example: The hero wins the war but loses everything they fought for.

**Negative → Positive Reversal:**
What seemed hopeless transforms. Despair becomes hope. Defeat becomes victory. Example: The underdog overcomes all obstacles.

**Ironic Reversal:**
The protagonist gets what they consciously desired but it's not what they actually needed. Or they achieve their goal but at a cost greater than the goal's worth. The reversal comments on the protagonist's journey and the story's themes.

### The Key Image: Concentrated Meaning

The key image is a single vivid image or moment that concentrates all the story's meaning. It often appears at the climax or resolution.

Examples:
- The empty chair at the dinner table
- The hero walking away from the burning house
- The closed door
- The photograph of the lost loved one
- The silhouette against the sunset

The key image doesn't explain the story's meaning; it embodies it. Readers remember images more vividly than plot points. A strong key image lingers after the story ends.

## The Inside-Out Writing Method

McKee's practical approach to story creation, designed to generate deep, authentic material:

### Phase 1: Research & Imagination

Accumulate life material through observation, experience, reading, and imagination. Fill notebooks with:

- Observations of real people and situations
- Dialogue overheard in public
- "What if" explorations
- Character fragments
- Emotional moments that moved you
- Contradictions and paradoxes

This is not yet story. It's raw material—a stockpile of authentic human behavior and incident. The more specific and truthful your observations, the richer your raw material.

### Phase 2: Step-Outline

Reduce all material to one-sentence scene descriptions. Write these on index cards—one card per scene.

The step-outline should capture:
- What happens (action)
- What changes (value turn)
- Why it matters (thematic resonance)

All in one sentence.

Example: "Sarah discovers her husband's affair and must decide whether to confront him."

The step-outline is a skeleton. It's not prose. It's a map of turning points.

### Phase 3: Treatment

Expand the step-outline into paragraphs. For each scene or sequence, write a paragraph describing:
- What happens (action)
- How it feels (emotional tone)
- What characters think and feel (subtext)
- Why it matters (thematic weight)

The treatment is still not dialogue or full prose, but it's more substantial than an outline. It helps you discover the emotional and thematic truth of scenes.

### Phase 4: Discover Your Story Climax

Before writing, be absolutely clear on what your climax is. Work backward:

- What choice must your protagonist make?
- What crisis forces that choice?
- What complications lead to the crisis?
- What inciting incident begins the chain?

Once you know your climax, every prior scene should genuinely lead toward it. No scene should feel arbitrary.

### Phase 5: Write

With deep knowledge of character, the skeleton of scenes, the emotional/thematic truth (treatment), and the climax clearly envisioned, dialogue and prose emerge naturally.

Writing at this stage is not discovery—it's execution. You're writing the story you've already thought through deeply. This produces prose that serves the story rather than digressing into indulgence.

## Character Through Conflict

Characters aren't revealed through exposition or introspection. They're revealed through the choices they make under pressure.

### Conscious Desire vs. Unconscious Desire (Character Arc)

- **Conscious Desire:** What the character openly pursues; what they think they want
- **Unconscious Desire:** What the character actually needs; what they're truly after beneath awareness

Great stories create tension between these desires:

Example: The protagonist consciously pursues revenge (plot) but unconsciously seeks forgiveness (character arc). As they pursue revenge, they discover it won't give them what they actually need.

The character arc is completed when the protagonist either achieves their unconscious desire or learns why they can't.

### Antagonistic Forces at Multiple Levels

Characters are defined not by who they are but by what they oppose them:

**Inner Antagonism:** The protagonist's own contradictions, fears, and self-deceptions. They are their own worst enemy.

**Personal Antagonism:** Opposition from other characters—an antagonist, rival, or love interest who represents incompatible values or desires.

**Extra-Personal Antagonism:** Opposition from society, nature, circumstance, or fate. The protagonist struggles against systems, environment, or luck.

Strong stories activate antagonism at all three levels simultaneously.

### The Principle of Antagonism

*A protagonist is only as compelling as the forces opposing them.*

If the antagonist is weak, the protagonist's struggle is boring. If the obstacles are easily overcome, we don't care about the journey. The worth of the protagonist's effort is directly proportional to the power of what opposes them.

This applies to all story types:
- In action stories: the antagonist must be as skilled and resourceful as the protagonist
- In dramas: the internal antagonism must be as powerful as external circumstances
- In literary fiction: the social/circumstantial antagonism must be genuinely formidable

### Character Creation: The Inside-Out Approach

Rather than building characters from external traits outward, build from internal contradictions inward:

1. **What does this character consciously want?**
2. **What do they actually need (and don't know)?**
3. **What internal contradiction creates this gap?**
4. **What's a choice they've made to avoid their true need?**
5. **What antagonistic forces will force them to confront themselves?**

This approach generates characters with psychological depth and organic character arcs.

## Dialogue Principles

Dialogue is one of the most difficult elements of writing. McKee offers clear principles:

### Write Dialogue Last

Don't write dialogue while structuring the story. First:
- Understand the scene's purpose and value turn
- Know what each character wants in the scene
- Understand the subtext and emotional truth
- Know how the dialogue will function (exposition, revelation, complication, etc.)

With this deep understanding, authentic dialogue emerges. Writing dialogue first (before understanding scene function) produces exposition-heavy, mechanical exchanges.

### Each Character Must Have a Distinct Voice

Voice includes:
- Vocabulary range (educated vs. colloquial)
- Sentence structure (short and clipped vs. long and flowing)
- Speech rhythms and cadence
- Recurring words or phrases (tics)
- What they reveal and what they hide
- Attitude toward the world

A reader should recognize who's speaking without attribution. If three characters sound identical, you need to differentiate them.

### Subtext Over Exposition

Characters rarely say exactly what they mean. They speak around the truth, hint at it, deny it, or express it indirectly.

Example (bad dialogue with no subtext):
> "I love you, but I'm angry because you forgot our anniversary."

Example (dialogue with subtext):
> "So what time are you coming home?"
> "Late. Work's crazy."
> "Right. Work."

The second version communicates the same information (plus subtext of hurt and resentment) without stating it directly. The reader fills in the emotional truth.

Exposition-heavy dialogue sounds artificial. Characters don't explain themselves to each other; they have conversations where meaning lies beneath the surface.

### Dialogue Is Action

Dialogue is not characters talking. It's characters doing something *through* talking—pursuing a desire, defending a position, winning an argument, avoiding a topic, seeking approval.

Each line of dialogue should be a character's action in response to another character's line. Not:

> "Where were you last night?" he asked.
> "I was at the office," she said.

But something like:

> "Where were you last night?"
> "The office was backed up."

The second character doesn't simply answer; they justify or deflect. That's action. That's dialogue that reveals character and drives conflict.
