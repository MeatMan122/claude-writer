## 1. Character vs. Characterization

### The Distinction

**Characterization** is the sum of all observable qualities: age, IQ, sex, style of speech, gesture, dress, education, occupation, personality, values, attitudes. This singular assemblage of traits makes each person unique — but it is not *character*.

**True character** is revealed only through the choices a person makes under pressure. The greater the pressure, the deeper the revelation, the truer the choice to the character's essential nature. (McKee, *Story*, p. 101)

A person's mask — how they present to the world — may be utterly different from who they are when it counts. A charming socialite may be ruthless when cornered. A gruff loner may risk everything for a stranger. You cannot know which until you see them choose.

### Why This Matters for Fiction

When characterization and true character match perfectly — when inner life and outer appearance are "like a block of cement, of one substance" — the role becomes a list of predictable behaviors. Shallow, nondimensional people may exist, but they are boring. (McKee, *Story*, p. 103)

The revelation of deep character in contrast or contradiction to characterization is fundamental to all fine storytelling. Life teaches us this: what *seems* is not what *is*. (McKee, *Story*, p. 103)

### Checklist: Character vs. Characterization

For each major character, define:
- [ ] **The mask**: What observable traits does this character present to the world? How do they want to be seen?
- [ ] **The crack**: What choice under pressure would reveal something beneath that mask?
- [ ] **The gap**: Is there a meaningful distance between who they appear to be and who they truly are? If not, add one.
- [ ] **The pressure**: What specific scenario would force this gap open?

## 2. The Pressure Test

### Core Principle

Pressure is essential. Choices made when nothing is at risk mean little. If a character chooses to tell the truth when telling a lie would gain him nothing, the choice is trivial. But if the same character insists on telling the truth when a lie would save his life, we sense that honesty is at the core of his nature. (McKee, *Story*, p. 101)

### The Burning Bus Test

McKee illustrates this with two strangers on a highway — a domestic worker (an illegal immigrant) and a wealthy neurosurgeon — who witness a school bus crash and burst into flames. Both could rationalize driving past. Both have everything to lose by stopping. Under this extreme pressure, their true natures emerge — and what emerges may contradict everything their characterizations suggest. The domestic worker might be braver than the surgeon. The surgeon's hands, which save lives in controlled settings, might freeze. Or both might rush in, revealing an identical humanity beneath utterly different surfaces. (McKee, *Story*, pp. 101-102)

### Practical Application: Discovery Through Pressure

Characters are not discovered by filling out profile sheets. They are discovered by designing scenes that force them into dilemmas.

**During ideation**: For each major character, sketch at least one hypothetical dilemma — a moment where they must choose between two things they value. Their choice reveals who they are.

**During detailed outlining**: For each major character, ensure at least one scene exists whose primary purpose is to pressure-test the character — to crack the mask and reveal true nature.

**During writing**: When a character faces a choice, don't default to the obvious option. Ask: what would the most revealing choice be? What choice would surprise the reader while feeling inevitable?

### Checklist: Pressure Test

- [ ] Does each major character face at least one genuine dilemma (not a choice between good and bad, but between two goods or two evils)?
- [ ] Does the story contain at least one moment where a character's choice under pressure contradicts their established characterization?
- [ ] Are the dilemmas escalating? Early choices should cost less than late ones. The climactic choice should cost everything.

## 3. Character Arc as Value Change

### Core Principle

The finest writing not only reveals true character but arcs or changes that inner nature, for better or worse, over the course of the telling. (McKee, *Story*, p. 104)

### How Arcs Work

McKee illustrates this with *The Verdict*: Frank Galvin begins as a corrupt, bankrupt, self-destructive drunk. His characterization peels back to reveal this. Then the case arrives — and his choices in pursuing it transform him. The legal battle changes him into a sober, ethical, excellent attorney — the kind of man he was before he lost his will to live. (McKee, *Story*, p. 104)

And with *Hamlet*: First, characterization is laid out (melancholy, confused). Second, true nature is revealed through choices (intelligent, cautious, passionate, rash — all in contradiction). Third, the story puts greater and greater pressure on him, forcing more difficult choices. Fourth, by the climax these choices have profoundly changed the character: his wars come to an end, his lively intelligence ripens into peaceful wisdom. (McKee, *Story*, p. 105)

### The Arc Template

For each protagonist or major character with an arc:

```
Character: [Name]
Begins believing: [Value/worldview at story's start]
Key pressures that challenge this belief:
  1. [First pressure — relatively low cost]
  2. [Escalating pressure — higher cost]
  3. [Crisis pressure — maximum cost]
Ends believing: [New value/worldview, or the same value held at a deeper level]
The turning point: [The specific moment the old belief fails them]
```

### Types of Arc

- **Positive arc**: Character grows, gains self-knowledge, becomes more capable or more human (Frank Galvin in *The Verdict*)
- **Negative arc**: Character degrades, loses humanity, descends into corruption or madness (Michael Corleone in *The Godfather*)
- **Flat arc with deepening**: Character's core belief is tested and survives, but they now hold it with earned conviction rather than naivete
- **Ironic arc**: Character achieves their conscious desire but loses what they actually needed (or vice versa)

### Checklist: Character Arc

- [ ] Does the protagonist end the story in a different inner state than they began?
- [ ] Is the change earned through escalating choices under escalating pressure?
- [ ] Can you point to the specific moment the old belief/value failed the character?
- [ ] Does the climactic choice complete the arc?

## 4. Dimension Through Contradiction

### Core Principle

"Dimension" is the least understood concept in character. Decorating a protagonist with quirks does not open character and draw empathy. Rather, eccentricities may close him off and keep us at a distance. (McKee, *Story*, p. 377-378)

**Dimension means contradiction**: either within deep character (guilt-ridden ambition) or between characterization and deep character (a charming thief). These contradictions must be *consistent* — it doesn't add dimension to portray a guy as nice throughout, then in one scene have him kick a cat. (McKee, *Story*, p. 378)

### Hamlet as Example

Consider Hamlet, the most complex character ever written. He isn't three-dimensional but "ten, twelve, virtually uncountably dimensional." To Ophelia he's first loving and tender, then callous, even sadistic. He's courageous, then cowardly. At times he's cool and cautious, then impulsive and rash. Hamlet is ruthless and compassionate, proud and self-pitying, witty and sad, weary and dynamic, lucid and confused, sane and mad — a living contradiction of almost any human quality we could imagine. (McKee, *Story*, p. 378)

### How to Create Dimensional Characters

The protagonist must be the most dimensional character in the cast — the most contradictory, the most surprising in their range of behavior. If a character can be summarized in a single adjective ("brave," "kind," "smart"), they lack dimension.

**Method**: For each major character, identify at least one consistent contradiction:

| Surface trait | Hidden counter-trait | When the counter-trait emerges |
|--------------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| Example: Gentle, soft-spoken | Capable of sudden, decisive violence | When someone threatens those he protects |
| Example: Ruthlessly ambitious | Genuinely guilt-ridden | Alone, late at night, in moments of quiet |
| Example: Intellectually fearless | Emotionally cowardly | When intimacy is offered |

### Diagnostic: The One-Adjective Test

If you can describe a character with a single word and nothing feels lost, the character lacks dimension. Fix it:
- "Brave" → Brave in battle, cowardly in love
- "Kind" → Kind to strangers, cruel to family
- "Smart" → Intellectually brilliant, emotionally obtuse

The contradiction doesn't need to be extreme. Subtle contradictions can be more interesting than dramatic ones.

### Checklist: Dimension

- [ ] Can each major character be described with a single adjective? If yes, they need a contradiction.
- [ ] Are the contradictions consistent (not random acts that break character)?
- [ ] Does the story contain scenes that bring out both sides of the contradiction?
- [ ] Is the protagonist the most dimensional character in the cast?

## 5. Cast Design

### Core Principle

In essence, the protagonist creates the rest of the cast. All other characters exist because of the relationship they strike to the protagonist and the way each helps delineate the protagonist's complex nature. Imagine a cast as a kind of solar system with the protagonist as the sun, supporting roles as planets around the sun, bit players as satellites — all held in orbit by the gravitational pull of the star at the center. (McKee, *Story*, p. 379)

### The Solar System Method

A four-dimensional protagonist needs a cast designed to bring out those dimensions. Supporting characters should provoke different facets of the protagonist:

- **Character A** provokes the protagonist's sadness and cynicism
- **Character B** brings out the protagonist's witty, hopeful side
- **Character C** inspires loving and courageous emotions
- **Character D** forces the protagonist to cower in fear, then strike out in fury

The creation and design of characters A, B, C, and D is dictated by the needs of the protagonist. They are what they are principally to make clear and believable, through action and reaction, the complexity of the central role. (McKee, *Story*, p. 379)

### Scaling Dimension Across the Cast

- **Protagonist**: Most dimensional (multiple consistent contradictions)
- **Major supporting roles**: Two-dimensional (one consistent contradiction — e.g., "outwardly beautiful and loving / inwardly grotesque as choices under pressure reveal cold, mutated desires")
- **Minor supporting roles**: May be one-dimensional but should still have one freshly observed trait that makes them worth encountering
- **Bit parts**: Deliberately flat, but not dull. Give each a single vivid detail.

### The Central Question Test

State the story's central question (e.g., "Is revenge justified?" or "Can love survive betrayal?"). Each major character should embody a different answer:

| Character | Their answer to the central question |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|
| Protagonist | [Evolves over the story] |
| Antagonist | [The opposing answer] |
| Ally | [A sympathetic alternative] |
| Foil | [A cautionary version] |

No two characters should hold the same answer. If they do, one may be redundant — or they need differentiation in *how* they arrived at their answer.

### Checklist: Cast Design

- [ ] Does every major character exist in relationship to the protagonist?
- [ ] Does each supporting character draw out a different facet of the protagonist?
- [ ] Is the protagonist the most dimensional character?
- [ ] Does each major character represent a different response to the story's central question?
- [ ] Are supporting characters scaled appropriately (two-dimensional, not flat, but not competing with the protagonist for complexity)?

## 6. Conscious vs. Unconscious Desire

### Core Principle

The protagonist has a conscious desire — a known need or goal, an object of desire. If you could pull your protagonist aside, whisper in his ear, "What do you want?" he would have an answer: "I'd like X today, Y next week, but in the end I want Z." (McKee, *Story*, p. 138)

However, the most memorable, fascinating characters tend to have not only a conscious but an unconscious desire. Although these complex protagonists are unaware of their subconscious need, the audience senses it, perceiving in them an inner contradiction. The conscious and unconscious desires of a multidimensional protagonist *contradict* each other. What he believes he wants is the antithesis of what he actually but unwittingly wants. (McKee, *Story*, pp. 138-139)

### How This Creates Ironic Structure

The tension between conscious and unconscious desire is the engine of ironic storytelling:

- **Conscious desire drives the plot**: What the character openly pursues, the goals they set, the actions they take
- **Unconscious desire drives the character arc**: What they actually need, the transformation they resist, the truth they can't see
- **The climax resolves both**: Either the character achieves their conscious desire but realizes it's not what they needed, or they sacrifice their conscious desire to fulfill their unconscious need, or they get neither (tragedy)

### Template: Desire Mapping

```
Character: [Name]
Conscious desire (what they pursue): [External goal]
Unconscious desire (what they need): [Internal need — contradicts conscious]
How they contradict: [The conscious pursuit actively prevents the unconscious need]
Resolution: [Which desire wins? At what cost?]
```

**Example**: A detective consciously pursues justice for a murder victim. Unconsciously, she needs to forgive herself for failing to save her own sister years ago. Her relentless pursuit of justice is actually avoidance of self-forgiveness — she can't stop working because stopping means facing her own guilt.

### Checklist: Desire Structure

- [ ] Does the protagonist have a clear conscious desire the audience can articulate?
- [ ] Does the protagonist have an unconscious desire that contradicts the conscious one?
- [ ] Does the story's structure force these two desires into collision?
- [ ] Does the climax resolve (or deliberately fail to resolve) both desires?

## 7. The Principle of Antagonism Applied to Character

### Core Principle

A protagonist is only as compelling as the forces opposing them. If the antagonist is weak, the protagonist's struggle is boring. If obstacles are easily overcome, the journey doesn't matter.

### Three Levels of Antagonism

Strong stories activate antagonism at all three levels simultaneously:

**Inner antagonism**: The protagonist's own contradictions, fears, and self-deceptions. They are their own worst enemy. This is the character's internal dimension warring with itself.

**Personal antagonism**: Opposition from other characters — the antagonist, rival, or love interest who represents incompatible values or desires. These characters should not be evil for evil's sake; they should have their own logic, their own desires, their own story in which they are the protagonist.

**Extra-personal antagonism**: Opposition from society, nature, institutions, circumstance, or fate. The character struggles against systems and environments that are indifferent to their desires.

### Designing Antagonism for Character Revelation

Each level of antagonism should force a different facet of the character to emerge:

- **Inner conflict** reveals what the character fears about themselves
- **Personal conflict** reveals what the character values when forced to choose between people
- **Extra-personal conflict** reveals how the character responds to forces beyond their control

### The Antagonist as Dark Mirror

The most effective antagonists embody the protagonist's unconscious desire, or represent what the protagonist could become if they made different choices. The antagonist is not the opposite of the protagonist — they are a *distorted reflection*.

### Checklist: Antagonism

- [ ] Does the protagonist face opposition at all three levels (inner, personal, extra-personal)?
- [ ] Does each level of antagonism reveal a different aspect of the protagonist's character?
- [ ] Does the antagonist have their own coherent desires and logic?
- [ ] Is the antagonist at least as formidable as the protagonist?
- [ ] Could you write a version of this story from the antagonist's POV where they're the hero?

## 8. Structure and Character Are the Same Thing

### Core Principle

The debate over whether "plot" or "character" is more important is specious, because structure is character and character is structure. They cannot be separated. (McKee, *Story*, p. 100)

**The function of STRUCTURE** is to provide progressively building pressures that force characters into more and more difficult dilemmas where they must make more and more difficult risk-taking choices and actions, gradually revealing their true natures, even down to the unconscious self. (McKee, *Story*, p. 105)

**The function of CHARACTER** is to bring to the story the qualities of characterization necessary to convincingly act out choices. The character must be credible: young enough or old enough, strong or weak, worldly or naive, educated or ignorant, generous or selfish, witty or dull, in the right proportions, for the audience to believe the character could and would do what he does. (McKee, *Story*, p. 106)

### The Interlock

If you change event design (structure), you change character — because the character is defined by how they respond to events. If you change deep character, you must reinvent the structure — because the events must now create pressures appropriate to the new nature. (McKee, *Story*, p. 106)

This means:
- You cannot design a plot without knowing who your characters are
- You cannot design characters without knowing what pressures they'll face
- The two must be developed simultaneously, in conversation with each other
- When something feels wrong with the plot, the fix may be in the character (and vice versa)

### Practical Implication for Fiction Forge

During outlining: never design structure in isolation from character, or characters in isolation from structure. Every structural beat should be checked against character ("Would this person actually make this choice?") and every character development should be checked against structure ("Does this trait change how they respond to the pressures I've designed?").

### Checklist: Structure-Character Interlock

- [ ] For every major structural beat: would this specific character make this specific choice, given their established nature?
- [ ] For every character trait: does the structure contain events that test, reveal, or transform this trait?
- [ ] If you changed the protagonist's core nature, would the plot need to change? (If not, the character isn't driving the structure)
- [ ] If you changed a major plot event, would the character need to respond differently? (If not, the event isn't pressuring the character)

## 9. The Audience Bond: Empathy, Not Sympathy

### Core Principle

The audience's emotional involvement is held by the glue of empathy. Empathy is not the same as sympathy. Sympathy means likable — we want them as friends. Empathy means "like me" — deep within the protagonist, the audience recognizes a certain shared humanity. (McKee, *Story*, p. 141)

Likability is merely an aspect of characterization: it's no guarantee of audience involvement. The audience identifies with deep character, with innate qualities revealed through choice under pressure. (McKee, *Story*, p. 142)

Even the most unsympathetic character can be made empathetic. Macbeth butchers a kindly old King, murders two servants, kills his best friend, orders the assassination of women and children. He's a ruthless killer — yet in Shakespeare's hands he becomes a tragic, empathetic hero. How? By giving Macbeth a conscience. His guilt-ridden soliloquies let the audience think: "I feel bad when I do bad things too. He's like me." (McKee, *Story*, p. 142)

### Practical Application

Do not confuse "likable" with "compelling." A protagonist does not need to be nice, kind, attractive, or admirable. They need to be *human* — they need at least one quality the audience recognizes in themselves.

Methods for creating empathy with difficult characters:
- Give them a conscience (they feel guilt, doubt, or conflict about their own actions)
- Give them a desire the audience shares (love, freedom, justice, survival)
- Show them as victims of injustice before showing them as perpetrators
- Reveal vulnerability beneath strength, or strength beneath vulnerability

### Checklist: Audience Bond

- [ ] Would the audience understand (not approve of, but understand) why the protagonist makes their key choices?
- [ ] Does the protagonist have at least one quality the audience can recognize in themselves?
- [ ] If the protagonist does terrible things, is there a human reason the audience can grasp?

## Quick Reference: Key Principles

1. **Characterization is the mask; character is revealed through choice under pressure.** The gap between them creates depth.
2. **Pressure is essential.** Trivial choices reveal nothing. Design dilemmas that cost something real.
3. **Character arcs are value changes.** The protagonist begins believing X and ends believing Y. The climax completes the transformation.
4. **Dimension means contradiction.** If a character can be captured in one adjective, they lack dimension. Add a consistent counter-trait.
5. **The cast is a solar system.** Every character exists to draw out a different dimension of the protagonist.
6. **Conscious desire drives plot; unconscious desire drives arc.** The tension between them is the engine of story.
7. **Three levels of antagonism** (inner, personal, extra-personal) should all be active, each revealing different facets of character.
8. **Structure and character are inseparable.** Change one, you must change the other. Design them together.
9. **Empathy, not sympathy.** The audience doesn't need to like the protagonist — they need to recognize shared humanity through a conscience, a desire, or a vulnerability.

## Diagnostic Quick Reference

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| Character feels generic | Characterization and true character are identical | Add a gap — make their true nature contradict their surface |
| Character feels flat | Single-adjective description | Add a consistent contradiction |
| Character choices feel predictable | Not enough pressure | Raise the stakes; force a genuine dilemma |
| Character arc feels unearned | Not enough escalating pressure | Add intermediate choices that cost progressively more |
| Supporting characters feel interchangeable | Not designed in relation to protagonist | Redesign each to draw out a different dimension |
| Antagonist feels cardboard | No coherent desires of their own | Give the antagonist their own logic and their own story |
| Audience doesn't care about protagonist | Mistaking sympathy for empathy | Add conscience, shared desire, or visible vulnerability |
| Plot and character feel disconnected | Designed separately | Check interlock — every event should test character; every character trait should affect response to events |
